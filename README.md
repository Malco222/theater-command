# Theater Command 1939-1945 (working title)

A grand strategy game of the Second World War in Europe and the Mediterranean: a turn-based campaign map on top, real-time battles underneath, joined by one contract called the Battle Ticket. The design framework (v9) is the source of truth for what the game is.

This repository holds **Phase 1** of the framework's roadmap: the campaign rules and auto-resolve on a simple browser map, built so the same rules and data can be carried into the Unreal Engine 5 build in Phase 2.

## What works today

**The campaign prototype** (open `dist/play.html` after a build)

- Europe, North Africa and the Middle East from Ireland to the Volga and from Norway to the Sahara in 373 regions and 22 seas, generated from real coastlines (Natural Earth, public domain). Moscow, Stalingrad, the Caucasus oilfields, the Crimea and Turkey are on the map, and so are French North Africa, Libya, Egypt and the Suez Canal, the Levant, Iraq, Gibraltar, Malta, Crete, Cyprus and Rhodes.
- 30 nations with their September 1939 armies as corps on the map, reinforcements by month, and replacement pools fed by industry.
- Monthly turns starting September 1939. Each corps gets four moves a month; fast troops in a fresh offensive get more.
- Supply traced from each nation's own capital, industry and depots. Armies that are cut off fight at half strength and waste away.
- Battles settled at once from a Battle Ticket (forces, terrain, forts, supply, air, commanders, tempo, direction of attack) with the framework's victory levels. Fortifications only count against the direction they face, so the Maginot Line can be outflanked.
- Nations ask for terms when they have lost too much of their capital, territory and army. France's fall creates Vichy; eastern Poland goes to the Soviet Union under the 1939 pact; sea-supplied armies can be lifted off from a port.
- Computer opponents that follow history's plan as data (`data/campaign/plan-historical.json`). The plan sets intentions only; every region still has to be won.
- A war economy. Factories earn production points every month (half speed at peace, half value on occupied ground). Losses are made good first; what is left buys new infantry, mountain, motorized and armoured corps and fortifications, which take months to arrive. Computer-run nations start ordering once their scripted mobilisation is over. All costs are data (`data/campaign/production.json`).
- The war at sea. Eighteen fleets start the war in their 1939 bases, each a count of battleships, carriers, cruisers, destroyers and submarines under a named admiral. A fleet patrols a sea to hold it, raids shipping, or lies in port to refit. Fleets that meet fight a fleet action settled from its own Battle Ticket, and aircraft flying from the coast count toward holding a sea, so the narrow waters belong to whoever owns the airfields. The naval rules are data (`data/campaign/navy.json`, `data/campaign/seas.json`).
- The war in the air. Twenty-two air fleets, each a count of fighters and bombers at the airfields of one region under a named commander: the Luftflotten, Fighter Command's groups, Bomber Command, the French air zones and the Soviet district air forces. Left alone a fleet supports the armies and defends the sky within reach of its fighters. Given a mission it fights a month-long air campaign, settled from its own Battle Ticket: win the sky over a region, bomb its industry by day or by night, or strike at shipping in a sea. The air rules are data (`data/campaign/air.json`).
- Range, radar and airfields. Fighters are at full effect to 60% of their range and useless beyond it, so bombers sent further go alone and suffer. Radar gets every defending fighter up in time; a defender without it meets raids with 70% of his fighters. The airfields of one region serve about 1,500 aircraft, and fleets packed in beyond that cannot all fly. Moving base costs readiness, and so does every month of operations.
- Aircraft and pilots. Factories and flying schools deliver on history's schedule into a reserve that tops the fleets up each month, resting fleets first. A new fighter needs a pilot. A pilot shot down over his own ground usually flies again; one shot down over the enemy's does not.
- The sky. An attacker wins the sky over a region when the fighters he has left at the end of the month outweigh, by 1.65 to 1, what the defender can still put up once his losses are made good. It lasts a month. An army attacking there has the air on its side, and no invasion fleet can cross to a coast while enemy fighters still hold the sky over it.
- Bombing cuts the victim's next month of production. Strikes on shipping sink warships at sea and coastal trade.
- The Battle of Britain. On history's plan the Luftwaffe fights over the Channel in July 1940, goes for the airfields of Kent and Hampshire in August, turns on London in September and goes over to the night Blitz in the autumn.
- Sea transport that can be intercepted. Troopships need sea lift and a route through seas the enemy has not closed. A crossing through contested water costs men, and an army overseas is supplied only while a port and an open sea link it to home.
- Landings from the sea. Corps in a port can be put ashore on an enemy coast up to three seas away. Troops storming a defended beach fight at 60% strength, unless they land in the very month war is declared, when surprise makes up the difference. Enemy fleets in harbour nearby put to sea against an invasion, and a landing cannot be attempted at all under a sky the enemy's fighters still hold. That is what makes Operation Sea Lion wait on the air battle.
- Norway, April 1940. Germany declares war on Denmark and Norway and lands at Oslo, Bergen, Trondheim and Narvik under cover of its Battle Fleet; the Home Fleet comes out to meet it and an Allied expedition goes ashore, then leaves when France is in danger.
- The convoy war. German submarines and raiders sink shipping on the Atlantic routes every month, and every ton lost cuts British production. Escorts, patrolling fleets and air cover cut the losses and sink U-boats. The U-boat arm grows on history's schedule.
- Swedish iron ore reaches Germany across the Baltic in summer and through Narvik when the Gulf of Bothnia freezes. In the winter months it arrives only while Narvik is in hands that are not at war with Germany, and losing it costs German industry.
- The war overseas. North Africa, the Levant and the island bases are colonies, and the rules treat them that way. A nation stands or falls by its home country, so losing Libya does not bring Italy nearer to asking for terms, and troops overseas are not counted in the army that defends the homeland. New corps are raised at home and sent out; history's expeditions (the Australians, the Afrika Korps, the Eighth Army's two corps) arrive in the month they did, at a friendly port, and only if the war they were sent for is being fought. When France falls the Army of Africa stays at its posts under Vichy. Battles overseas roll their own dice, so a fight in the desert never changes the luck of the war in Europe.
- The desert. There is one road, no water and nothing to live on, so an army in North Africa is only as strong as the port behind it. Every corps' supply line is counted from the nearest working port, a step for every region of desert: Tripoli and Alexandria are great harbours, Benghazi and Tobruk small ones that start as if two regions out. The longer the line, the weaker the attack, the thinner the defence, the slower the replacements and the shorter each bound forward into enemy ground. An ally's port feeds the corps who came to fight beside him. A fortified port keeps two months of stores after the sea behind it is closed, which is what lets Tobruk stand a siege. No railway crosses the desert. All of it is data (`data/campaign/desert.json`).
- Open desert. Infantry on foot outside prepared works has no flank to anchor on: against an attack that includes tanks or lorried troops it defends at 65%, and when it is driven off it loses more men than troops who can drive away. The Battle Ticket says so.
- The Libya convoys. Every man, tank and litre of fuel for the Axis army in Africa crosses the Central Mediterranean. Malta hunts those convoys for as long as it holds out, harder while it is itself supplied, and every tenth of the cargo sunk makes the Axis supply line in Libya half a region longer. With Malta in Axis hands the route is safe.
- The desert war on history's plan (`data/campaign/theatres.json`). An army overseas is run by a plan of its own, separate from its nation's plan in Europe: the Italians cross into Egypt in September 1940 and stop at Sidi Barrani, Operation Compass throws them back across Cyrenaica that winter, Rommel lands at Tripoli in February 1941 and wins it all back in the spring while Tobruk holds out behind him, and Operation Crusader lifts the siege at the end of the year.
- Side theatres. A theatre is a list of regions with plans of its own (`data/campaign/theatres.json`), and it can be on the mainland as well as overseas. A nation with a plan there runs every corps it has on that ground by that plan and the rest of its army by its main plan; neither army takes orders meant for the other, neither marches onto the other's ground, and battles in a theatre roll their own dice and their own generals' luck. Formations can be lent to a theatre for a set time and go back to the main army when it is up, so the panzer divisions that overrun Yugoslavia are in Poland again for Barbarossa.
- Greece and the Balkans on history's plan. Italy attacks out of Albania in October 1940 and is stopped in the mountains of Epirus all winter, the spring offensive included. Britain garrisons Crete and sends an expedition to Thessaly. In April 1941 officers in Belgrade overthrow the regent, Germany comes through Bulgaria, Romania and Hungary, and Yugoslavia and Greece fall within weeks. The occupation troops stay; the armour leaves.
- Havens. Troops lifted off a coast in a theatre are taken to that theatre's haven while it is in friendly hands and has room, not all the way home: the British expedition leaves Greece for Crete, and for Egypt only when Crete goes too. An expedition's plan can tell it to get out once a named place has fallen.
- Airborne troops. An airborne corps takes off from a friendly airfield and comes down on any enemy coast across one sea, with no ships and no regard for who holds the water, unless the enemy holds the sky over it. It fights without its heavy weapons, at 90%, and the transport aircraft can carry one such assault a month. Crete falls this way in May 1941, at a price.
- Shared shipping. In a side theatre a port that any ally's ships can reach feeds the troops who hold it, so a German garrison on a Greek island lives on Italian convoys.
- Saved games. The whole campaign, including the exact state of the random number stream, is written to the browser as you play, and the start screen offers to continue it. A loaded game plays out exactly as the unsaved one would have.
- Diplomacy on the framework's seven-step alignment track. Every neutral has a public opinion that drifts back to its own leaning and bends toward whoever is winning the war. A country that leans toward a bloc trades with it; one that is fully won over joins it. Players spend political capital to court neutrals, and moves that match public opinion go further.
- History as events (`data/campaign/diplomacy.json`): the Soviet occupation of the Baltic states and Bessarabia, the Vienna Award, the Tripartite Pact. Each fires once, in its window, and only if its conditions still hold. If France does not fall, the Danube states do not join the Axis.
- The eastern front of 1941. Germany's plan runs from the build-up in Poland through Barbarossa, Smolensk, Kiev and the drive on Moscow to the winter line; Romania marches with it, and Finland, Hungary and Slovakia declare war. The Soviet plan holds, then strikes back in December.
- Supply that thins out with distance. Inside the Soviet Union every army's supply line is measured from its railheads: the longer it is, the weaker its attacks, the thinner its defence, the slower its replacements and the more each step forward costs. Captured railways are converted over a few months. All of it is data (`data/campaign/east.json`).
- Mud and winter. Mud in November and April slows every army in the east; each season is counted from the month after it sets in, so March is still frozen ground that carries an army. From December to February armies without winter equipment fight and march badly on Soviet ground and lose men to the cold. The Red Army and the Finns do not.
- The Red Army's mobilisation. From the month it is attacked the Soviet Union raises new rifle corps every month, and defence lines are dug around Leningrad and then Moscow.
- The eastern front of 1942 and the winter after it. Germany takes its tanks out of the quiet parts of the line in the spring and gathers them in the south. Case Blue opens in June: Voronezh, then the Don and Rostov in July, the land bridge to the Volga in August and the Caucasus as far as Maikop and the Terek by the autumn. Stalingrad is assaulted across the land bridge and holds. In November the Red Army, which has stood quiet all summer, breaks the Romanian armies on both flanks and closes the ring; the army on the Volga starves and lays down its arms in January. The German army in the Caucasus gets out to the Kuban coast in time. The Soviet winter offensive takes Voronezh, Rostov and Kursk, and in March the German counter-stroke retakes Kharkov before the thaw stops everything.
- The land bridge. Between the bend of the Don and the Volga there is one region, Kalach, and it is the only way to Stalingrad from the west. The Don flank above it and the Kalmyk steppe below it are separate regions, so an army on the land bridge is cut off the moment both go.
- Orders a plan can give (`data/campaign/plan-historical.json`). Besides where to attack and at what odds, a month of history's plan can now say: gather so many tank and lorried corps at a place by rail (`concentrate`), keep so many corps massed at a place whatever else happens (`staging`), leave a stretch of the line to an ally and take your own corps out once he is there (`entrust`), pay a price of its own for one prize (`oddsFor`, which is how Stalingrad is assaulted at odds the plan would refuse anywhere else), stand quiet and attack nothing (`quiet`), and give ground up and march out before the road home is cut (`abandon`). The side panel explains each one in plain words to a player whose nation has such an order that month, and the player is free to ignore it.
- Germany's allies on the Don. The Hungarian 2nd Army, the Italian 8th Army and the Romanian 3rd and 4th Armies arrive through the summer of 1942 and take over the Don front from Voronezh to the Kalmyk steppe, so that the German corps can go on to the Volga. They go in beside whoever stands there, however crowded the place, and what is left of the Hungarians and Italians goes home in the spring of 1943. Italy follows Germany into the war in the east by an event, and only into a war Germany has actually begun.
- News of an encirclement. The newsreel reports the month a ring closes around three corps or more, with the men cut off, and reports the end as one piece of news when they lay down their arms.
- Limited manpower. However rich the treasury, an army gets back only about three men in a hundred of its peak strength each month, shared along the front.
- Staffs that learn. The Red Army of 1941 can bring only eight corps into one battle and does not renew a failed assault in the same month. Defenders who hold their ground lose fewer men than those who are driven off it.
- Computer generals who bring reserves up behind a spearhead, garrison what they have taken, leave a besieged city alone when the plan says to starve it out, counter-attack ground they have just lost, and close any gap in a line they are standing on.
- Generals. The real commanders of 1939 start the war at the head of their corps, and others wait for a command. A general gains experience from every battle, and each level brings a skill point for one of four skills: offense, defense, logistics and leadership. Traits are earned from how he actually fights: three victories that end in surrender make an encirclement master, three positions held against a stronger attacker make an iron defender, three costly failed assaults make a butcher. Generals can be wounded, captured or killed, and unknown officers make their names in battle and rise from the ranks. The player assigns, relieves, promotes and dismisses them and spends their skill points; the computer does the same for its own. Everything a general adds is shown on the Battle Ticket. All of it is data (`data/campaign/commanders.json`).
- Research and doctrine. The five great powers each have eight design teams, one to a branch: tanks, infantry weapons and guns, army doctrine, fighters, bombers, the navy, radar and signals, and war production. Research runs on history's clock: a branch with one team gets every new design in the month history got it, from the 50 mm Panzer III and the Spitfire Mk V to the Panther, the Lancaster and the T-34/85. A second or third team brings a branch's designs forward, a branch with no team stands still, and extra teams can be founded with production points. What counts in battle is how far a branch is ahead of or behind its own history: each design in service early is worth 5 to 8 percent to the corps, fleets, fighters, bombers or factories that use it until history catches up, and each one missed costs the same. Doctrine is each army's own: Blitzkrieg, Deep Battle, Superior Firepower, Methodical Battle, War of Rapid Decision. The computer leaves one team on every branch, so its nations fight exactly as they did. The Research tab shows every branch as a strip of the whole war, with history's dates, the designers' progress and today marked on it. All of it is data (`data/campaign/research.json`).
- The eve of war. Germany moves first every month, and on history's plan Poland is beaten inside the first one. A player who takes Poland therefore places the army on 31 August 1939, before anyone moves: marches and rail moves on Polish ground, nothing across the frontier, and no month passes. Left strung along the frontier as it was, the army is destroyed in September; drawn back round Warsaw, it holds the capital through the German attack and the player fights on.
- Play Germany, France, the United Kingdom or Poland from September 1939, Germany or the United Kingdom from July 1940 with the Battle of Britain about to begin, the United Kingdom or Italy from September 1940 with the Italian army on the Egyptian frontier, Greece or Italy from October 1940 with the Italian army about to cross the Greek frontier, Germany or the Soviet Union from June 1941, or Germany or the Soviet Union from June 1942 with Case Blue about to open. You can also watch history unfold from any of the six dates.

**The exit tests** from the framework run as automated tests, with every nation on the historical plan across 20 different seeds.

- "France falls in about six weeks": Poland falls in about four weeks and France six to eight weeks after the May 1940 attack, with most of the British Expeditionary Force lifted off first. Norway is invaded in April and holds out into the summer.
- "A believable Barbarossa" (the Phase 3 test, run early against the campaign rules): Minsk falls in June 1941, Smolensk in July, Kiev in September and Kharkov in October. Moscow and Leningrad hold. In December the Red Army strikes back and wins ground, and the German army is hurt but not broken. Through March 1942 Germany loses roughly 0.5 to 1.0 million men and the Soviet Union 2.6 to 3.5 million.
- "Case Blue and Stalingrad" (the second year of the Phase 3 test, run early against the campaign rules, through May 1943): the tanks are in the south by May 1942; the Soviet offensive at Kharkov that month fails; Voronezh falls by July, Rostov and the Don bend by August, the land bridge by September, Maikop by November; Grozny, Astrakhan and Stalingrad never fall, and the assaults on Stalingrad cost the attacker more than the defender. The Red Army makes no attack from June to October. In November or December at least three German corps and 90,000 men are cut off on the land bridge, nobody gets out, and the newsreel reports both the ring and the end. The Caucasus army loses no more than two corps coming out. By the end of February Voronezh, Rostov and Kursk are Soviet again and the Axis has lost over 300,000 men since November; at the end of March Kharkov is German, and April is quiet.

- "Played historically, the Luftwaffe fails to win air superiority by mid-September" (the Phase 2 test, run early against the campaign rules): on history's plan the sky over Kent, Hampshire and London is never won, and it is never close. From July to November 1940 Fighter Command loses about 1,000 to 1,200 fighters and 520 to 620 pilots and ends as strong as it began; the Luftwaffe loses about 1,800 to 2,100 aircraft. History's figures to the end of October were 1,023 fighters, 544 pilots and 1,887 aircraft. Britain is not invaded.
- The what-if is tested as well. A player who brings all five air fleets within reach of Kent, spreads them over the airfields of Calais, Flanders, Lille, the Somme and Rouen, rests them for a month and then goes for No. 11 Group's airfields month after month wins the sky over Kent in September or October. The two western air fleets alone never do, and neither does everything crammed into Calais. Once the sky is won the troopships can cross, and the Royal Navy still sinks part of the force on the way.
- "The desert war" (part of the Phase 3 Mediterranean test, run early against the campaign rules): the Italians reach Sidi Barrani in the autumn of 1940 and stop; by February 1941 the British hold Tobruk, Benghazi and Agedabia and more than half the Italian army in Libya is gone; by May the Axis is back on the Egyptian frontier with Tobruk still British behind it; the siege lasts through October; by the end of December 1941 Tobruk is relieved and the frontier cleared. Egypt never falls and Malta holds. The general who led a panzer division in France is the same man who lands at Tripoli.
- "Greece and the Balkans" (the second part of the Phase 3 Mediterranean test, run early against the campaign rules): Italy declares war on Greece in October 1940 and its first assault on Epirus fails; the Greeks still hold Epirus and Salonika in March 1941, and the mountain war has cost Italy more than twice what it cost Greece. Germany attacks in April: Belgrade falls and Yugoslavia asks for terms that month, Athens by the end of May, for under 40,000 German casualties. The British expedition is lifted off to Crete, and home when Crete falls to the airborne corps, which loses more than 2,000 men taking it. By the end of June no German tank is left in the Balkans, Barbarossa starts on time, and no corps of either German army has strayed onto the other's ground.
- Research moves that balance, and that is tested too. A Britain that put second teams on fighters and radar in 1939 keeps its sky even against all five air fleets. A Germany that put three teams on fighters in 1939 has the Bf 109F in the summer of 1940 and the Fw 190 by October, and wins the sky over Kent with the two western air fleets alone.

Where 1941 still differs from history: Odessa falls a month or two early, Rostov is never reached, Soviet losses are lower than the real ones, and Finland, Hungary and Slovakia declare war but only defend. In the desert, Operation Crusader goes better for the British than it did: in about two wars in three they run on to Agedabia or El Agheila with the Axis army badly cut down, and Rommel's counter-stroke of January 1942 seldom wins much of it back. In the Balkans, Albania is one region, so the Greek counter-offensive that took Koritsa is not drawn: the front stays on the frontier. Crete falls in nearly every trial war, where the real battle was a close-run thing, and the troops sent to Greece are extra to the desert army rather than taken from it, so Rommel's first offensive is not helped by their going. Italy and Bulgaria gain nothing in April 1941, and there are no occupation zones: Germany holds all of Yugoslavia and Greece.

Where 1942 differs from history: the army that is lost is cut off on the land bridge west of Stalingrad, not in the city, which the Germans never enter; it is three to five corps and 100,000 to 270,000 men where the real pocket held about 250,000, and it is gone in January, not on 2 February. There is no relief attempt and no airlift. The Soviet offensive at Kharkov in May 1942 costs 60,000 to 70,000 men, not a quarter of a million. Kharkov changes hands in February and March in about half the trial wars and is never lost in the rest. Nothing is planned for the north and the centre in 1942, so there is no fighting at Rzhev or Demyansk and no attempt to lift the siege of Leningrad. In about one war in ten the German army of 1941 never reaches Kharkov or the Donets, and its 1942 offensive starts from further back.

**The flight model** for the air battle (`src/air/flight.ts`) reproduces the documented speed, climb and turn of the Spitfire, Hurricane, Bf 109, Bf 110, Do 17 and He 111 from their physical data, and is covered by tests.

## What is not here yet

- The gray-box air battle screen (the other half of Phase 1). The flight model, scenario data and pilot logic exist; the battle loop and the command screen do not.
- The rest of the war in the air. The computer flies air offensives only where history's plan has one, which today means Germany against Britain in 1940 and 1941: it does not fly a British night offensive against Germany, does not react to a player's air offensive by moving its fighter groups, and never attempts Sea Lion itself. Support for the armies still comes from each nation's air rating, not from where its air fleets stand. There are no aircraft types and no carrier aircraft, and air transport is only the one airborne assault a month.
- The rest of research: the computer never changes its research priorities, there is no retooling cost when a new design enters production, no captured or Lend-Lease equipment, no stolen technology, and none of the framework's superweapon projects. The United States and Japan are not in the game yet, so Carrier Warfare and Strategic Bombing are not yet doctrines of their own.
- The rest of the war at sea: building warships to order (today new ships arrive on history's schedule), carrier strikes and port raids, and mines. Sea battles are settled at once from fleet strength; there is no tactical naval battle.
- The rest of the Mediterranean: the fighting in Syria and Iraq, East Africa, the Malta convoys, and the partisan war in Yugoslavia and Greece. Malta's garrison is supplied like any other port and its bite on the Libya convoys is one number; there are no air fleets in the Mediterranean yet, so there is no air siege of the island, and the computer never tries to take it. The computer's fleets do not fight for the Libya route.
- The rest of diplomacy: changing your own nation's side, treaties, espionage, and computer-run nations that court neutrals themselves. Today the computer follows history's events and the drift of the war.
- Everything after the spring of 1943 in the east, and after 1941 in the desert: Gazala, El Alamein, Torch, Tunisia and Kursk are not planned yet. History's plans stop there; the game goes on, but computer-run nations only hold their lines. The United States is not in the war.
- The rest of the commander system: skills and traits for admirals and air commanders (today they gain levels from their battles, and an admiral can be lost with his flagship), command limits, rivalries and politics between generals, and staff college. Generals lead single corps today, because the prototype has no armies above the corps yet.
- Graphics. This is deliberately a staff map. The Total War and Company of Heroes level of presentation arrives with the Unreal Engine 5 build.
- Borders are approximate: regions are grown from seed points, not traced from 1939 maps.

## Run it

```
npm install
npm test            # flight model, war economy, research, diplomacy, eastern front, desert and Balkans rules, generals, the war at sea, the war in the air, the plans' orders of 1942, saved games and the six exit tests
npm run build       # writes dist/play.html, a single file you can open in a browser
npm run history     # prints a full computer-vs-computer war; add "sweep 20" to compare seeds
npm run geo         # regenerates the maps from data/campaign/regions.mjs
```

## How it is laid out

| Path | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| `data/` | Moddable data: nations, regions, seas, starting armies, fleets and air fleets, the historical plans in Europe and in the side theatres, the eastern front and desert rules, generals, research, aircraft |
| `src/core/` | Shared deterministic core: seeded random numbers, maths, the Battle Ticket contract |
| `src/campaign/game.ts` | Campaign rules: war, movement, supply, weather, battles, mobilisation, capitulation |
| `src/campaign/economy.ts` | Production points, replacements, new corps and fortifications |
| `src/campaign/research.ts` | Research and doctrine: design teams, history's clock, what a lead is worth |
| `src/campaign/diplomacy.ts` | The alignment track, courting, and history's events |
| `src/campaign/navy.ts` | The war at sea: fleets, sea control, fleet actions, convoys, troopships and landings |
| `src/campaign/air.ts` | The war in the air: air fleets, range, radar, missions, the sky, bombing and strikes |
| `src/campaign/commanders.ts` | Generals: experience, skills, traits, wounds and capture, promotion |
| `src/campaign/save.ts` | Saved games |
| `src/campaign/ai.ts` | The computer's generals |
| `src/campaign/turn.ts` | The monthly turn and the player's orders |
| `src/campaign/ui/` | The campaign screen |
| `src/air/` | Flight model and air battle groundwork |
| `tests/` | Rule tests: flight model, war economy, research, the war at sea, the war in the air, saved games, diplomacy, the eastern front, the desert and the colonies, the Balkans and airborne troops, the eve of war, the orders a plan can give, generals |
| `tests/exit/` | The framework's exit tests |
| `tools/` | Map generation and the history runner |

Everything in the simulation is deterministic: the same seed and the same orders always replay the same war. That is what will make saved games, replays and multiplayer possible, and it is why the rules can be ported to Unreal without changing their results.

## Roadmap (from the framework)

1. **Phase 1, now.** Campaign rules and auto-resolve in a browser map (done, and now deepening with production, saved games, diplomacy, the eastern front of 1941, generals, the war at sea, the war in the air, research, the Mediterranean and North Africa, Greece and the Balkans, and the eastern front of 1942 and Stalingrad), and a gray-box air battle with 300 aircraft (paused).
2. **Phase 2.** The Battle of Britain as a vertical slice in Unreal Engine 5: one campaign turn, one playable air battle, the full Battle Ticket loop, on licensed or commissioned art. Its campaign half (radar, airfields, the aircraft and pilot pipeline, Dowding's groups under Park and Leigh-Mallory, the Channel battles and Sea Lion as a what-if) is already playable on the browser map, and its exit test passes there.
3. **Later phases.** Land battles, the war at sea, the full campaign systems, the other theatres.
