# Theater Command 1939-1945 (working title)

A grand strategy game of the Second World War in Europe and the Mediterranean: a turn-based campaign map on top, real-time battles underneath, joined by one contract called the Battle Ticket. The design framework (v9) is the source of truth for what the game is.

This repository holds **Phase 1** of the framework's roadmap: the campaign rules and auto-resolve on a simple browser map, built so the same rules and data can be carried into the Unreal Engine 5 build in Phase 2.

## What works today

**The campaign prototype** (open `dist/play.html` after a build)

- Europe, North Africa and the Middle East from Ireland to the Volga and from Norway to the Sahara in 373 regions and 22 seas, generated from real coastlines (Natural Earth, public domain). Moscow, Stalingrad, the Caucasus oilfields, the Crimea and Turkey are on the map, and so are French North Africa, Libya, Egypt and the Suez Canal, the Levant, Iraq, Gibraltar, Malta, Crete, Cyprus and Rhodes.
- 31 nations, 30 with their September 1939 armies as corps on the map, reinforcements by month, and replacement pools fed by industry. The thirty-first is the United States, which is off the map and comes in later.
- Monthly turns starting September 1939. Each corps gets four moves a month; fast troops in a fresh offensive get more.
- Supply traced from each nation's own capital, industry and depots. Armies that are cut off fight at half strength and waste away.
- Battles settled at once from a Battle Ticket (forces, terrain, forts, supply, air, commanders, tempo, direction of attack) with the framework's victory levels. Fortifications only count against the direction they face, so the Maginot Line can be outflanked.
- Nations ask for terms when they have lost too much of their capital, territory and army. France's fall creates Vichy; eastern Poland goes to the Soviet Union under the 1939 pact; sea-supplied armies can be lifted off from a port.
- Computer opponents that follow history's plan as data (`data/campaign/plan-historical.json`). The plan sets intentions only; every region still has to be won.
- A war economy. Factories earn production points every month (half speed at peace, half value on occupied ground). Losses are made good first; what is left buys new infantry, mountain, motorized and armoured corps and fortifications, which take months to arrive. Computer-run nations start ordering once their scripted mobilisation is over. All costs are data (`data/campaign/production.json`).
- The war at sea. Eighteen fleets start the war in their 1939 bases, each a count of battleships, carriers, cruisers, destroyers and submarines under a named admiral. A fleet patrols a sea to hold it, raids shipping, or lies in port to refit. Fleets that meet fight a fleet action settled from its own Battle Ticket, and aircraft flying from the coast count toward holding a sea, so the narrow waters belong to whoever owns the airfields. The naval rules are data (`data/campaign/navy.json`, `data/campaign/seas.json`).
- The war in the air. Twenty-two air fleets, each a count of fighters and bombers at the airfields of one region under a named commander: the Luftflotten, Fighter Command's groups, Bomber Command, the French air zones and the Soviet district air forces. Left alone a fleet supports the armies and defends the sky within reach of its fighters. Given a mission it fights a month-long air campaign, settled from its own Battle Ticket: win the sky over a region, bomb its industry by day or by night, or strike at shipping in a sea. The air rules are data (`data/campaign/air.json`).
- Range, radar and airfields. Fighters are at full effect to 60% of their range and useless beyond it, so bombers sent further go alone and suffer. Radar gets every defending fighter up in time; a defender without it meets raids with 70% of his fighters. The airfields of one region serve about 1,500 aircraft, and fleets packed in beyond that cannot all fly. Moving base costs readiness, and so does every month of operations.
- Aircraft and pilots. Factories and flying schools deliver on history's schedule into a reserve that tops the fleets up each month, resting fleets first. A new fighter needs a pilot. A pilot shot down over his own ground usually flies again; one shot down over the enemy's does not.
- The sky. An attacker wins the sky over a region when the fighters he has left at the end of the month outweigh, by 1.65 to 1, what the defender can still put up once his losses are made good. It lasts a month. An army attacking there has the air on its side, and no invasion fleet can cross to a coast while enemy fighters still hold the sky over it.
- Bombing cuts the victim's next month of production. Strikes on shipping sink warships at sea and coastal trade.
- The Battle of Britain. On history's plan the Luftwaffe fights over the Channel in July 1940, goes for the airfields of Kent and Hampshire in August, turns on London in September and goes over to the night Blitz in the autumn.
- Sea transport that can be intercepted. Troopships need sea lift and a route through seas the enemy has not closed. A crossing through contested water costs men, and an army overseas is supplied only while a port and an open sea link it to home.
- Landings from the sea. Corps in a port can be put ashore on an enemy coast up to three seas away. Troops storming a defended beach fight at 60% strength, unless they land in the very month war is declared, when surprise makes up the difference. Enemy fleets in harbour nearby put to sea against an invasion, and a landing cannot be attempted at all under a sky the enemy's fighters still hold. That is what makes Operation Sea Lion wait on the air battle.
- Norway, April 1940. Germany declares war on Denmark and Norway and lands at Oslo, Bergen, Trondheim and Narvik under cover of its Battle Fleet; the Home Fleet comes out to meet it and an Allied expedition goes ashore, then leaves when France is in danger.
- The convoy war. German submarines and raiders sink shipping on the Atlantic routes every month, and every ton lost cuts British production. Escorts, patrolling fleets and air cover cut the losses and sink U-boats. The U-boat arm grows on history's schedule.
- Swedish iron ore reaches Germany across the Baltic in summer and through Narvik when the Gulf of Bothnia freezes. In the winter months it arrives only while Narvik is in hands that are not at war with Germany, and losing it costs German industry.
- The war overseas. North Africa, the Levant and the island bases are colonies, and the rules treat them that way. A nation stands or falls by its home country, so losing Libya does not bring Italy nearer to asking for terms, and troops overseas are not counted in the army that defends the homeland. New corps are raised at home and sent out; history's expeditions (the Australians, the Afrika Korps, the Eighth Army's two corps) arrive in the month they did, at a friendly port, and only if the war they were sent for is being fought. When France falls the Army of Africa stays at its posts under Vichy. Battles overseas roll their own dice, so a fight in the desert never changes the luck of the war in Europe.
- The desert. There is one road, no water and nothing to live on, so an army in North Africa is only as strong as the port behind it. Every corps' supply line is counted from the nearest working port, a step for every region of desert: Tripoli and Alexandria are great harbours, Benghazi and Tobruk small ones that start as if two regions out. The longer the line, the weaker the attack, the thinner the defence, the slower the replacements and the shorter each bound forward into enemy ground. An ally's port feeds the corps who came to fight beside him. A fortified port keeps two months of stores after the sea behind it is closed, which is what lets Tobruk stand a siege. No railway crosses the desert. All of it is data (`data/campaign/desert.json`).
- Open desert. Infantry on foot outside prepared works has no flank to anchor on: against an attack that includes tanks or lorried troops it defends at 65%, and when it is driven off it loses more men than troops who can drive away. The Battle Ticket says so.
- The Libya convoys. Every man, tank and litre of fuel for the Axis army in Africa crosses the Central Mediterranean. Malta hunts those convoys for as long as it holds out, harder while it is itself supplied, and every tenth of the cargo sunk makes the Axis supply line in Libya half a region longer. With Malta in Axis hands the route is safe.
- The desert war on history's plan (`data/campaign/theatres.json`). An army overseas is run by a plan of its own, separate from its nation's plan in Europe: the Italians cross into Egypt in September 1940 and stop at Sidi Barrani, Operation Compass throws them back across Cyrenaica that winter, Rommel lands at Tripoli in February 1941 and wins it all back in the spring while Tobruk holds out behind him, and Operation Crusader lifts the siege at the end of the year.
- The desert in 1942. The Axis army beaten in Operation Crusader no longer stands and dies: it marches out of Cyrenaica to El Agheila in December 1941. In January Rommel comes back to Agedabia and Benghazi and the line settles at Gazala, where both armies build up through the spring. In May he turns the line at Bir Hakeim, in June Tobruk falls and the British go back to El Alamein, the last position before Alexandria. The Axis tries the Alamein line in July and is thrown back. Montgomery lands in August with a fresh armoured corps, the armour is brought forward in September while the Axis digs in behind minefields, and in October the British break the line and chase what is left of the Axis army back across Libya: Tobruk and Benghazi in November, El Agheila by the end of the year, with Tripoli still to take.
- A plan that depends on how the war has gone. In a side theatre several phases of a plan may cover the same months, and a phase can be made to wait until the enemy holds a named place (`ifLost`); the first phase in the list that fits is the one followed. The Axis leaves Cyrenaica only once the frontier is lost, the British leave Gazala for El Alamein the month Bir Hakeim or Tobruk falls, and the Axis leaves its line before El Alamein only once that line is broken.
- Falling back on a base. Each army in a theatre has a port it falls back on (`bases`): told to give ground up, its corps march toward it, and where the one road is full of its own troops those in front go a stage further so that those behind can get out. An army overseas has no railway, so when its plan gathers the armour the tanks march up from as far as a month's marching reaches, and infantry makes room for them.
- Works that change with the month (`works` in `data/campaign/desert.json`). The mines and wire of Tobruk were lifted to build the Gazala line, so from May 1942 it is a fortress of level 1, not 2; the Axis digs in before El Alamein in September. Each happens only if the place is still in the hands that did it, and the newsreel says so.
- The United States. It stands aside until December 1941, when Pearl Harbor brings it into the war against Germany and Italy. It is a power off the map: it has no regions and no capital, its ships come in from the North Atlantic, its industry counts though no region holds it, and it raises nothing on the map. Its army is the one history sent: II Corps under Fredendall and the Western Task Force under Patton sail for Torch in November 1942, and more divisions follow. Having no airfields on the map of its own, its air force flies only for its own army.
- Torch and Tunisia on history's plan. In November 1942 Britain and the United States go to war with Vichy France and land in Morocco and Algeria from Gibraltar. The French fight, and when December opens Darlan's ceasefire brings North Africa and the Levant over to the Allies, while Germany and Italy occupy the rest of France and Corsica and fly troops into Tunis. The British dash for Tunis in December is beaten off. Tripoli falls in January and the Axis army goes back past it to the Mareth Line. In February two panzer divisions strike the Americans in front of Tebessa and are thrown back. The Mareth Line is broken in March, Sfax falls in April and Tunis in May, where the Axis army in Africa lays down its arms. Anderson, Fredendall, Patton, von Arnim and Messe are named at the head of their formations.
- Events that wake a nation or divide one (`data/campaign/diplomacy.json`). An event can bring in a country that has stood aside (`wake`), and can end one and share out what it held (`partition`): each share goes to the first of its takers still in the war. Events can wait on a war between two nations, on a nation still standing aside, and on one or all of a list of places being in a bloc's hands. That is how Pearl Harbor, Darlan's ceasefire and the news that Africa is clear are written.
- More orders for an army overseas. A phase of a theatre's plan can name the place its army falls back on for the months it runs (`base`), which is how the Axis goes back to Mareth in January and into the Tunis bridgehead in the spring. The force a plan keeps at a place joins no attack elsewhere, so the garrison of the Mareth Line stays out of the stroke at Kasserine. A landing can carry other corps from the same quay in one lift (`with`), and tries each beach on its list in turn, passing over one it cannot storm at the plan's odds.
- Older saved games still load. A country added to the game since the save was written takes its place in the order of play, and the month goes on with whoever was moving.
- Side theatres. A theatre is a list of regions with plans of its own (`data/campaign/theatres.json`), and it can be on the mainland as well as overseas. A nation with a plan there runs every corps it has on that ground by that plan and the rest of its army by its main plan; neither army takes orders meant for the other, neither marches onto the other's ground, and battles in a theatre roll their own dice and their own generals' luck. Formations can be lent to a theatre for a set time and go back to the main army when it is up, so the panzer divisions that overrun Yugoslavia are in Poland again for Barbarossa.
- Greece and the Balkans on history's plan. Italy attacks out of Albania in October 1940 and is stopped in the mountains of Epirus all winter, the spring offensive included. Britain garrisons Crete and sends an expedition to Thessaly. In April 1941 officers in Belgrade overthrow the regent, Germany comes through Bulgaria, Romania and Hungary, and Yugoslavia and Greece fall within weeks. The occupation troops stay; the armour leaves.
- Havens. Troops lifted off a coast in a theatre are taken to that theatre's haven while it is in friendly hands and has room, not all the way home: the British expedition leaves Greece for Crete, and for Egypt only when Crete goes too. An expedition's plan can tell it to get out once a named place has fallen.
- Airborne troops. An airborne corps takes off from a friendly airfield and comes down on any enemy coast across one sea, with no ships and no regard for who holds the water, unless the enemy holds the sky over it. It fights without its heavy weapons, at 90%, and the transport aircraft can carry one such assault a month. Crete falls this way in May 1941, at a price.
- Shared shipping. In a side theatre a port that any ally's ships can reach feeds the troops who hold it, so a German garrison on a Greek island lives on Italian convoys.
- Saved games. The whole campaign, including the exact state of the random number stream, is written to the browser as you play, and the start screen offers to continue it. A loaded game plays out exactly as the unsaved one would have.
- Diplomacy on the framework's seven-step alignment track. Every neutral has a public opinion that drifts back to its own leaning and bends toward whoever is winning the war. A country that leans toward a bloc trades with it; one that is fully won over joins it. Players spend political capital to court neutrals, and moves that match public opinion go further.
- History as events (`data/campaign/diplomacy.json`): the Soviet occupation of the Baltic states and Bessarabia, the Vienna Award, the Tripartite Pact. Each fires once, in its window, and only if its conditions still hold. If France does not fall, the Danube states do not join the Axis.
- The eastern front of 1941. Germany's plan runs from the build-up in Poland through Barbarossa, Smolensk, Kiev and the drive on Moscow to the winter line; Romania marches with it, and Finland, Hungary and Slovakia declare war. The Soviet plan holds, then strikes back in December.
- Supply that thins out with distance. Inside the Soviet Union every army's supply line is measured from its railheads: the longer it is, the weaker its attacks, the thinner its defence, the slower its replacements and the more each step forward costs. Captured railways are converted over a few months. All of it is data (`data/campaign/east.json`).
- Mud and winter. Mud in November and April slows every army in the east; each season is counted from the month after it sets in, so March is still frozen ground that carries an army. From December to February armies without winter equipment fight and march badly on Soviet ground and lose men to the cold. The Red Army and the Finns do not.
- The Red Army's mobilisation. From the month it is attacked the Soviet Union raises new rifle corps every month, and defence lines are dug around Leningrad and then Moscow.
- The eastern front of 1942 and the winter after it. Germany takes its tanks out of the quiet parts of the line in the spring and gathers them in the south. Case Blue opens in June: Voronezh, then the Don and Rostov in July, the land bridge to the Volga in August and the Caucasus as far as Maikop and the Terek by the autumn. Stalingrad is assaulted across the land bridge and holds. In November the Red Army, which has stood quiet all summer, breaks the Romanian armies on both flanks and closes the ring; the army on the Volga starves and lays down its arms in January. The German army in the Caucasus gets out to the Kuban coast in time. The Soviet winter offensive takes Voronezh and Kursk; in February Germany gives up Rostov once the Caucasus army is through and holds the Mius in front of Stalino, and in March the German counter-stroke retakes Kharkov before the thaw stops everything. An army told to leave a salient makes room on the road for the corps behind it, so nobody is left at the far end because the next stage was full.
- Kursk and the road to the Dnieper, 1943. The spring is quiet. In June the Red Army digs three belts of defences round the Kursk salient and forms the Central and Voronezh Fronts and two tank armies inside it, with the Steppe Front and three more tank armies behind; Rokossovsky, Vatutin, Katukov, Rotmistrov and Rybalko are named at their head. Germany gathers its tanks at Orel and Kharkov and in July attacks the salient once, and is held. The fronts in the salient are then let loose on Orel, the reserves take Kharkov in August, and in September the German army gives up everything east of the Dnieper and mans the river from Kiev to Zaporozhye, while the Red Army takes Smolensk, Bryansk and the Donbas behind it. The Red Army is over the river in October and in Kiev in November or December. If it takes Zhitomir too, the panzers strike back there before the year is out.
- A plan can allow one assault and no more. Germany's plan for Kursk attacks the salient once; the army is not sent in a second time the same month, as it would be anywhere else.
- Sicily and Italy, 1943. Italy is a theatre of its own, with its own plan and its own dice. In June an Allied air command under Tedder forms at Malta and wins the sky over Sicily, because no invasion fleet can cross under enemy fighters. In July the Eighth Army under Montgomery and Leese lands in Sicily from Malta with the battle fleet covering it, and the Seventh Army under Patton and Bradley follows it ashore: twelve divisions, taken out of the armies in Africa. Guzzoni's Italians are driven across the strait, where Hube's panzer corps is waiting. Mussolini falls as August opens. As September opens Italy signs an armistice and leaves the war with its army, fleet and air force: Germany takes over Rome, the north and the Italian garrisons abroad, and the Allies get Sardinia and Corsica. That month the Germans go back to the mountains south of Rome and leave a rearguard at Naples, the Eighth Army crosses the strait and lands in the heel, and the Fifth Army under Clark lands at Salerno and takes Naples. The air command moves up to Foggia. In October German engineers dig the Gustav Line, and it holds to the end of the year with Rome behind it.
- Landing ships. Troops fighting their way off a beach count for 60 percent of their strength. From June 1943 British and American troops count for 80 percent, for the landing ships, amphibious lorries and naval gunfire they have by then. Who has them, and from when, is data (`data/campaign/navy.json`).
- Armies made from armies. A formation can be put together out of an army already overseas: the invasion force for Sicily takes its divisions out of the British and American armies in Africa, and they shrink by as much. A corps left with no divisions is struck off, and garrisons are never drawn on.
- The land bridge. Between the bend of the Don and the Volga there is one region, Kalach, and it is the only way to Stalingrad from the west. The Don flank above it and the Kalmyk steppe below it are separate regions, so an army on the land bridge is cut off the moment both go.
- Orders a plan can give (`data/campaign/plan-historical.json`). Besides where to attack and at what odds, a month of history's plan can now say: gather so many tank and lorried corps at a place by rail (`concentrate`), keep so many corps massed at a place whatever else happens (`staging`), leave a stretch of the line to an ally and take your own corps out once he is there (`entrust`), pay a price of its own for one prize (`oddsFor`, which is how Stalingrad is assaulted at odds the plan would refuse anywhere else), stand quiet and attack nothing (`quiet`), and give ground up and march out before the road home is cut (`abandon`). The same orders can be given to an army in a side theatre. The side panel explains each one in plain words to a player whose nation has such an order that month, at home or overseas, and the player is free to ignore it.
- Germany's allies on the Don. The Hungarian 2nd Army, the Italian 8th Army and the Romanian 3rd and 4th Armies arrive through the summer of 1942 and take over the Don front from Voronezh to the Kalmyk steppe, so that the German corps can go on to the Volga. They go in beside whoever stands there, however crowded the place, and what is left of the Hungarians and Italians goes home in the spring of 1943. Italy follows Germany into the war in the east by an event, and only into a war Germany has actually begun.
- News of an encirclement. The newsreel reports the month a ring closes around three corps or more, with the men cut off, and reports the end as one piece of news when they lay down their arms.
- Limited manpower. However rich the treasury, an army gets back only about three men in a hundred of its peak strength each month, shared along the front.
- Staffs that learn. The Red Army of 1941 can bring only eight corps into one battle and does not renew a failed assault in the same month. In June 1943 an event remakes it: its troops are better, its air force is the equal of Germany's, it can bring fourteen corps into a battle, renews its attacks and moves reserves by rail like any other army. An event of this kind can remake any nation's army, and it is written in the data: another takes the heart out of the Italian army in July 1943, once Tunis is lost, and its divisions fight at three fifths of what they were worth. Defenders who hold their ground lose fewer men than those who are driven off it.
- Computer generals who bring reserves up behind a spearhead, garrison what they have taken, leave a besieged city alone when the plan says to starve it out, counter-attack ground they have just lost, and close any gap in a line they are standing on.
- Generals. The real commanders of 1939 start the war at the head of their corps, and others wait for a command. A general gains experience from every battle, and each level brings a skill point for one of four skills: offense, defense, logistics and leadership. Traits are earned from how he actually fights: three victories that end in surrender make an encirclement master, three positions held against a stronger attacker make an iron defender, three costly failed assaults make a butcher. Generals can be wounded, captured or killed, and unknown officers make their names in battle and rise from the ranks. The player assigns, relieves, promotes and dismisses them and spends their skill points; the computer does the same for its own. Everything a general adds is shown on the Battle Ticket. All of it is data (`data/campaign/commanders.json`).
- Research and doctrine. The five great powers each have eight design teams, one to a branch: tanks, infantry weapons and guns, army doctrine, fighters, bombers, the navy, radar and signals, and war production. Research runs on history's clock: a branch with one team gets every new design in the month history got it, from the 50 mm Panzer III and the Spitfire Mk V to the Panther, the Lancaster and the T-34/85. A second or third team brings a branch's designs forward, a branch with no team stands still, and extra teams can be founded with production points. What counts in battle is how far a branch is ahead of or behind its own history: each design in service early is worth 5 to 8 percent to the corps, fleets, fighters, bombers or factories that use it until history catches up, and each one missed costs the same. Doctrine is each army's own: Blitzkrieg, Deep Battle, Superior Firepower, Methodical Battle, War of Rapid Decision. The computer leaves one team on every branch, so its nations fight exactly as they did. The Research tab shows every branch as a strip of the whole war, with history's dates, the designers' progress and today marked on it. All of it is data (`data/campaign/research.json`).
- The eve of war. Germany moves first every month, and on history's plan Poland is beaten inside the first one. A player who takes Poland therefore places the army on 31 August 1939, before anyone moves: marches and rail moves on Polish ground, nothing across the frontier, and no month passes. Left strung along the frontier as it was, the army is destroyed in September; drawn back round Warsaw, it holds the capital through the German attack and the player fights on.
- Play Germany, France, the United Kingdom or Poland from September 1939, Germany or the United Kingdom from July 1940 with the Battle of Britain about to begin, the United Kingdom or Italy from September 1940 with the Italian army on the Egyptian frontier, Greece or Italy from October 1940 with the Italian army about to cross the Greek frontier, Germany or the Soviet Union from June 1941, the United Kingdom or Italy from May 1942 with Rommel striking at the Gazala line, Germany or the Soviet Union from June 1942 with Case Blue about to open, the United Kingdom or Italy from November 1942 with the Torch convoys at Gibraltar, Germany or the Soviet Union from July 1943 with the panzers gathered on both sides of the Kursk salient, or the United Kingdom or Italy from the same month with the invasion fleet gathering at Malta. You can also watch history unfold from any of the nine dates, and from July 1943 with the map on either Kursk or Sicily.

**The exit tests** from the framework run as automated tests, with every nation on the historical plan across 20 different seeds.

- "France falls in about six weeks": Poland falls in about four weeks and France six to eight weeks after the May 1940 attack, with most of the British Expeditionary Force lifted off first. Norway is invaded in April and holds out into the summer.
- "A believable Barbarossa" (the Phase 3 test, run early against the campaign rules): Minsk falls in June 1941, Smolensk in July, Kiev in September and Kharkov in October. Moscow and Leningrad hold. In December the Red Army strikes back and wins ground, and the German army is hurt but not broken. Through March 1942 Germany loses roughly 0.5 to 1.0 million men and the Soviet Union 2.6 to 3.5 million.
- "Case Blue and Stalingrad" (the second year of the Phase 3 test, run early against the campaign rules, through May 1943): the tanks are in the south by May 1942; the Soviet offensive at Kharkov that month fails; Voronezh falls by July, Rostov and the Don bend by August, the land bridge by September, Maikop by November; Grozny, Astrakhan and Stalingrad never fall, and the assaults on Stalingrad cost the attacker more than the defender. The Red Army makes no attack from June to October. In November or December at least three German corps and 90,000 men are cut off on the land bridge, nobody gets out, and the newsreel reports both the ring and the end. The Caucasus army loses no more than two corps coming out. By the end of February Voronezh, Rostov and Kursk are Soviet again and the Axis has lost over 300,000 men since November; at the end of March Kharkov is German, and April is quiet.

- "Played historically, the Luftwaffe fails to win air superiority by mid-September" (the Phase 2 test, run early against the campaign rules): on history's plan the sky over Kent, Hampshire and London is never won, and it is never close. From July to November 1940 Fighter Command loses about 1,000 to 1,200 fighters and 520 to 620 pilots and ends as strong as it began; the Luftwaffe loses about 1,800 to 2,100 aircraft. History's figures to the end of October were 1,023 fighters, 544 pilots and 1,887 aircraft. Britain is not invaded.
- The what-if is tested as well. A player who brings all five air fleets within reach of Kent, spreads them over the airfields of Calais, Flanders, Lille, the Somme and Rouen, rests them for a month and then goes for No. 11 Group's airfields month after month wins the sky over Kent in September or October. The two western air fleets alone never do, and neither does everything crammed into Calais. Once the sky is won the troopships can cross, and the Royal Navy still sinks part of the force on the way.
- "The desert war" (part of the Phase 3 Mediterranean test, run early against the campaign rules): the Italians reach Sidi Barrani in the autumn of 1940 and stop; by February 1941 the British hold Tobruk, Benghazi and Agedabia and more than half the Italian army in Libya is gone; by May the Axis is back on the Egyptian frontier with Tobruk still British behind it; the siege lasts through October; by the end of December 1941 Tobruk is relieved and the frontier cleared. Egypt never falls and Malta holds. The general who led a panzer division in France is the same man who lands at Tripoli.
- "Gazala and El Alamein" (the desert in 1942, run early against the campaign rules, through January 1943): at the end of December 1941 the Axis stands at El Agheila with about 100,000 men; Agedabia and Benghazi are Axis again in January and the line stands at Gazala through April with no fighting in March or April; Bir Hakeim falls in May or June and Tobruk in June, and Gazala costs the British 54,000 to 77,000 men against 6,000 to 13,000; the Axis is at Mersa Matruh by July, attacks El Alamein in July or August and never takes it; at the end of September the British have 190,000 to 220,000 men in the desert against about 150,000, with Montgomery's armour at El Alamein; in October the Axis line is broken and from then to the end of the year the Axis loses 50,000 to 75,000 men against about 10,000; Tobruk and Benghazi are British in November and El Agheila in December, and Tripoli is still Axis when the year ends. Egypt behind the Alamein line never falls and Malta holds.
- "Torch and Tunisia" (the end in Africa, run early against the campaign rules, through June 1943): the United States comes into the war in December 1941 and has no army on the map before November 1942. That month three landings go ashore in Morocco and Algeria and none is thrown back, and the Americans take Casablanca by storm. With the ceasefire French North Africa is Allied, and Germany and Italy hold the rest of France, Corsica and Tunisia. The British try for Tunis in December and are beaten off, and Tunis holds until the spring. Tripoli falls in January or February 1943, and the Axis army gets away into Tunisia with more than 150,000 men. In February the panzers break the front before Tebessa at more than twice the cost to the Allies, over 3,000 of it American, and the ground is Allied again by the end of March. The Mareth Line goes in March or April and Sfax by the end of April. Tunis falls in May, no Axis soldier is left in Africa, more than 60,000 prisoners are taken in the spring, and the newsreel says Africa is clear. From November to May the Axis loses over 180,000 men, more than three times the Allied loss; the Americans lose 8,000 to 40,000 and the British 25,000 to 80,000. Gibraltar is never lost.
- "Kursk and the Dnieper" (the third year of the Phase 3 test, run early against the campaign rules, through December 1943): in most wars the summer opens with a Soviet salient at Kursk between Orel and Kharkov. June is quiet. The Red Army is remade that month, digs in at Kursk and has its two fronts and two tank armies inside the salient, and Germany has its tanks on both shoulders. In July Germany attacks Kursk once, with ten corps or more, loses over 45,000 men and is held; the assault is never renewed and Kursk is never lost. Orel is Soviet by the end of August and Kharkov by the end of September, and the Red Army has to fight for them. Smolensk, Bryansk, Poltava and Stalino are free by the end of September, with Kiev and Zaporozhye still German. Dnepropetrovsk and Zaporozhye fall in October. Kiev holds through October and is free by the end of December, with Gomel and usually Zhitomir. Germany ends the year still holding Vitebsk, Mogilev, Minsk, Novgorod, Pskov, Nikolaev, Odessa and the Crimea. From July to December each side loses several hundred thousand men, and the German army in the east shrinks by a fifth or more without being destroyed. Leningrad, Moscow, Stalingrad and Voronezh are never lost.
- "Sicily and Italy" (the Mediterranean in 1943, run early against the campaign rules, through December 1943): in June Italy still holds its own country and no German corps stands south of Tuscany. The Allied air command forms at Malta that month and wins the sky over Sicily. The Italian army loses heart as July opens. Twelve divisions leave the armies in Africa for Malta, the Eighth Army storms Sicily in July at odds between 1.2 and 2.6 to 1, nobody is thrown back into the sea, and both armies are on the island by the end of the month. The Germans hold the far side of the strait through August and never counter-attack. Mussolini falls as August opens and Italy leaves the war as September opens, with its army, fleet and air force; Germany holds Rome, Tuscany, the Marche, Lombardy and Liguria, and the Allies Sardinia and Corsica. By the end of September no German stands in Calabria, Apulia or Naples, and the toe and the heel are Allied. The Fifth Army takes Naples from the sea in September. The Gustav Line is dug in October with six German divisions or more on it, and Abruzzo, Rome and Tuscany are German at the end of December. The year ends with seven Allied corps or more in southern Italy facing five German ones, and the campaign has cost each side tens of thousands of men, not armies.
- "Greece and the Balkans" (the second part of the Phase 3 Mediterranean test, run early against the campaign rules): Italy declares war on Greece in October 1940 and its first assault on Epirus fails; the Greeks still hold Epirus and Salonika in March 1941, and the mountain war has cost Italy more than twice what it cost Greece. Germany attacks in April: Belgrade falls and Yugoslavia asks for terms that month, Athens by the end of May, for under 40,000 German casualties. The British expedition is lifted off to Crete, and home when Crete falls to the airborne corps, which loses more than 2,000 men taking it. By the end of June no German tank is left in the Balkans, Barbarossa starts on time, and no corps of either German army has strayed onto the other's ground.
- Research moves that balance, and that is tested too. A Britain that put second teams on fighters and radar in 1939 keeps its sky even against all five air fleets. A Germany that put three teams on fighters in 1939 has the Bf 109F in the summer of 1940 and the Fw 190 by October, and wins the sky over Kent with the two western air fleets alone.

Where 1941 still differs from history: Odessa falls a month or two early, Rostov is never reached, Soviet losses are lower than the real ones, and Finland, Hungary and Slovakia declare war but only defend. In the desert, the Axis army gets away from Operation Crusader to El Agheila stronger than the real one did, with about 100,000 men. In the Balkans, Albania is one region, so the Greek counter-offensive that took Koritsa is not drawn: the front stays on the frontier. Crete falls in nearly every trial war, where the real battle was a close-run thing, and the troops sent to Greece are extra to the desert army rather than taken from it, so Rommel's first offensive is not helped by their going. Italy and Bulgaria gain nothing in April 1941, and there are no occupation zones: Germany holds all of Yugoslavia and Greece.

Where 1942 differs from history: the army that is lost is cut off on the land bridge west of Stalingrad, not in the city, which the Germans never enter; it is three to five corps and 100,000 to 270,000 men where the real pocket held about 250,000, and it is gone in January, not on 2 February. There is no relief attempt and no airlift. The Soviet offensive at Kharkov in May 1942 costs 60,000 to 70,000 men, not a quarter of a million. Kharkov changes hands in February and March in about a third of the trial wars and is never lost in the rest. Nothing is planned for the north and the centre in 1942, so there is no fighting at Rzhev or Demyansk and no attempt to lift the siege of Leningrad. In about one war in ten the German army of 1941 never reaches Kharkov or the Donets, and its 1942 offensive starts from further back.

Where the desert of 1942 differs from history: the map has one region between El Alamein and Sidi Barrani, so the Axis line of the autumn stands at Mersa Matruh and the October battle carries that name on its Battle Ticket. The Axis army at El Alamein is about 150,000 men where the real one had some 116,000, and it tries the line in July and seldom again, where history saw a second attempt at Alam Halfa at the end of August. There is no army level above the corps, so Montgomery leads the armoured corps he arrives with. Land battles still take their air support from each nation's air rating, so the Battle Tickets of October give the sky to the Axis, which is wrong. The desert generals of 1940 and 1941 other than Rommel, Cruwell, O'Connor and Montgomery are still unnamed.

Where Torch and Tunisia differ from history: Algiers is too strongly held to storm, so the British land further east, at Constantine, and Algiers comes over with the ceasefire, which arrives when December opens, not after three days. The Levant passes to Britain at the same ceasefire, because the Syrian campaign of 1941 is not in the game. The French who came over fight as one corps under the British flag. Rommel stays in Africa to the end and can be taken prisoner there. After Kasserine the Americans mostly hold their front, and Sfax and Tunis fall to the British: there are no separate battles of El Guettar or Bizerte. The stroke at Kasserine is made by two divisions, and the British take the ground back the same month. American neutrality and Lend-Lease before Pearl Harbor are not modelled, American air power counts only in battles the Americans fight themselves, and the United States cannot be played.

Where 1943 in the east differs from history: the two sides lose about the same number of men from July to December, where the Red Army really lost several times what Germany did, because under these rules the side that wins a battle loses less. Citadel is one battle for the whole salient, decided in a month, so there is no Prokhorovka and no separate northern and southern thrust. Orel falls in July, a month early, and to the fronts from inside the salient. In about half the wars the Red Army walks into the Dnieper bend at Kirovograd in the autumn, two or three months early, because Germany has no infantry left to post there. Kiev falls in November in most wars and in December in the rest. The German stroke at Zhitomir only happens in the wars where the Red Army has the town when December opens, about one in five, and the town changes hands twice inside the month. The Kuban bridgehead, the Mius and the Nevel battles are not fought as such. When Italy leaves the war in September, the share of its air force that counted for the Axis in the east goes with it, which is worth a few percent to the Red Army from then on.

Where Sicily and Italy differ from history: Sicily is one region, so the five weeks of fighting for the island are one battle on the beaches, decided in July; the German divisions that really fought on the Etna line and got away over the strait in August stand in Calabria from the start and are never in that battle. The same is true at Salerno: the Fifth Army meets one panzer division there and takes the whole Naples region in September, where the city really fell on 1 October, and the German counter-attack on the beachhead is not fought. The dice decide very little in this campaign: all sixty test wars ran to the same calendar. After Naples nothing more is fought in 1943, where the Allies really spent November and December forcing the Volturno, the Sangro and the Bernhardt Line at heavy cost. The Italian fleet is laid up in port and does not sail to Malta. There is no Italian Social Republic, no Italian army fighting beside the Allies, no rescue of Mussolini, and no fighting between Germans and Italians in Rome, the Balkans or the Dodecanese. Corsica goes to Britain with Sardinia, because the Free French are not a separate army. The Mediterranean Air Command is British in the game: the American air forces that were half of it are not counted separately.

**The flight model** for the air battle (`src/air/flight.ts`) reproduces the documented speed, climb and turn of the Spitfire, Hurricane, Bf 109, Bf 110, Do 17 and He 111 from their physical data, and is covered by tests.

## What is not here yet

- The gray-box air battle screen (the other half of Phase 1). The flight model, scenario data and pilot logic exist; the battle loop and the command screen do not.
- The rest of the war in the air. The computer flies air offensives only where history's plan has one, which today means Germany against Britain in 1940 and 1941 and the Allies against Sicily in 1943: it does not fly a British night offensive against Germany, does not react to a player's air offensive by moving its fighter groups, and never attempts Sea Lion itself. Support for the armies still comes from each nation's air rating, not from where its air fleets stand. There are no aircraft types and no carrier aircraft, and air transport is only the one airborne assault a month.
- The rest of research: the computer never changes its research priorities, there is no retooling cost when a new design enters production, no captured or Lend-Lease equipment, no stolen technology, and none of the framework's superweapon projects. The United States has no design teams and Japan is not in the game yet, so Carrier Warfare and Strategic Bombing are not yet doctrines of their own.
- The rest of the war at sea: building warships to order (today new ships arrive on history's schedule), carrier strikes and port raids, and mines. Sea battles are settled at once from fleet strength; there is no tactical naval battle.
- The rest of the Mediterranean: the fighting in Syria and Iraq, East Africa, the Malta convoys, and the partisan war in Yugoslavia and Greece. Malta's garrison is supplied like any other port and its bite on the Libya convoys is one number; the only air fleet based in the Mediterranean is the Allied one formed for Sicily in June 1943, so there is no air siege of the island, and the computer never tries to take it. The computer's fleets do not fight for the Libya route.
- The rest of diplomacy: changing your own nation's side, treaties, espionage, and computer-run nations that court neutrals themselves. Today the computer follows history's events and the drift of the war.
- Italy after 1943: Anzio, Cassino and the road to Rome belong to 1944 and are not planned yet. A computer-run Italy has no plan of its own for the campaign: it defends its country by the general rules until the armistice.
- Everything after 1943. History's plans stop in December 1943; the game goes on, but computer-run nations only hold their lines.
- The rest of the United States: it is an army in North Africa and an industry off the map, and no more. It cannot be played, builds nothing, sends no Lend-Lease, and its neutrality before Pearl Harbor is not modelled. Japan and the Pacific are not in the game.
- The rest of the commander system: skills and traits for admirals and air commanders (today they gain levels from their battles, and an admiral can be lost with his flagship), command limits, rivalries and politics between generals, and staff college. Generals lead single corps today, because the prototype has no armies above the corps yet.
- Graphics. This is deliberately a staff map. The Total War and Company of Heroes level of presentation arrives with the Unreal Engine 5 build.
- Borders are approximate: regions are grown from seed points, not traced from 1939 maps.

## Run it

```
npm install
npm test            # flight model, war economy, research, diplomacy, eastern front, desert and Balkans rules, generals, the war at sea, the war in the air, the plans' orders of 1942 at home and overseas, the United States, Torch and Tunisia, Kursk and the Dnieper, Sicily and Italy, saved games and the ten exit tests
npm run build       # writes dist/play.html, a single file you can open in a browser
npm run history     # prints a full computer-vs-computer war; add "sweep 20" to compare seeds
npm run geo         # regenerates the maps from data/campaign/regions.mjs
```

## How it is laid out

| Path | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| `data/` | Moddable data: nations, regions, seas, starting armies, fleets and air fleets, the historical plans in Europe and in the side theatres, the eastern front and desert rules, generals, research, aircraft |
| `src/core/` | Shared deterministic core: seeded random numbers, maths, the Battle Ticket contract |
| `src/campaign/game.ts` | Campaign rules: war, movement, supply, weather, battles, mobilisation, capitulation |
| `src/campaign/economy.ts` | Production points, replacements, new corps and fortifications |
| `src/campaign/research.ts` | Research and doctrine: design teams, history's clock, what a lead is worth |
| `src/campaign/diplomacy.ts` | The alignment track, courting, and history's events |
| `src/campaign/navy.ts` | The war at sea: fleets, sea control, fleet actions, convoys, troopships and landings |
| `src/campaign/air.ts` | The war in the air: air fleets, range, radar, missions, the sky, bombing and strikes |
| `src/campaign/commanders.ts` | Generals: experience, skills, traits, wounds and capture, promotion |
| `src/campaign/save.ts` | Saved games |
| `src/campaign/ai.ts` | The computer's generals |
| `src/campaign/turn.ts` | The monthly turn and the player's orders |
| `src/campaign/ui/` | The campaign screen |
| `src/air/` | Flight model and air battle groundwork |
| `tests/` | Rule tests: flight model, war economy, research, the war at sea, the war in the air, saved games, diplomacy, the eastern front, the desert and the colonies, the Balkans and airborne troops, the eve of war, the orders a plan can give at home and overseas, the United States, Torch and Tunisia, Kursk and the Dnieper, Sicily and Italy, generals |
| `tests/exit/` | The framework's exit tests |
| `tools/` | Map generation and the history runner |

Everything in the simulation is deterministic: the same seed and the same orders always replay the same war. That is what will make saved games, replays and multiplayer possible, and it is why the rules can be ported to Unreal without changing their results.

## Roadmap (from the framework)

1. **Phase 1, now.** Campaign rules and auto-resolve in a browser map (done, and now deepening with production, saved games, diplomacy, the eastern front of 1941, generals, the war at sea, the war in the air, research, the Mediterranean and North Africa, Greece and the Balkans, the eastern front of 1942 and Stalingrad, the desert of 1942 and El Alamein, the United States, Torch and Tunisia, Kursk and the road to the Dnieper, and Sicily and Italy in 1943), and a gray-box air battle with 300 aircraft (paused).
2. **Phase 2.** The Battle of Britain as a vertical slice in Unreal Engine 5: one campaign turn, one playable air battle, the full Battle Ticket loop, on licensed or commissioned art. Its campaign half (radar, airfields, the aircraft and pilot pipeline, Dowding's groups under Park and Leigh-Mallory, the Channel battles and Sea Lion as a what-if) is already playable on the browser map, and its exit test passes there.
3. **Later phases.** Land battles, the war at sea, the full campaign systems, the other theatres.
