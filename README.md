# Theater Command 1939-1945 (working title)

A grand strategy game of the Second World War in Europe and the Mediterranean: a turn-based campaign map on top, real-time battles underneath, joined by one contract called the Battle Ticket. The design framework (v9) is the source of truth for what the game is.

This repository holds **Phase 1** of the framework's roadmap: the campaign rules and auto-resolve on a simple browser map, built so the same rules and data can be carried into the Unreal Engine 5 build in Phase 2.

## What works today

**The campaign prototype** (open `dist/play.html` after a build)

- Europe, North Africa and the Middle East from Ireland to the Volga and from Norway to the Sahara in 375 regions and 22 seas, generated from real coastlines (Natural Earth, public domain). Moscow, Stalingrad, the Caucasus oilfields, the Crimea and Turkey are on the map, and so are French North Africa, Libya, Egypt and the Suez Canal, the Levant, Iraq, Gibraltar, Malta, Crete, Cyprus and Rhodes.
- 31 nations, 30 with their September 1939 armies as corps on the map, reinforcements by month, and replacement pools fed by industry. The thirty-first is the United States, which is off the map and comes in later.
- Monthly turns starting September 1939. Each corps gets four moves a month; fast troops in a fresh offensive get more.
- Armies as pieces. Each nation's force in a region stands on the map as one piece: its flag of the time on a standard (the Kingdom of Italy's Savoy arms, the 48-star flag, the hammer and sickle; Germany flies the black, white and red tricolour with an iron cross, as most games of the period do), a small plate on the flag's corner when its strongest corps is armoured, motorized, cavalry, mountain or fortress troops, its senior general's name over it, a bar under it for the strength it has left. Click one of yours to pick it up: a tray along the bottom of the map shows the general and the army's corps as cards, and a click on a card leaves that corps behind. Right-click a region to march there or to plan an attack on it; the road the army would take is drawn as the pointer passes over, and the piece is seen to march. On a touch screen a tap or a press-and-hold does the same. Fleets and air fleets fly the same flag over their markers, and it stands beside each nation's name in the panels.
- The face-off. Before an attack the two armies are drawn up against each other: each side's general, men, divisions and corps as cards, the ground between them, and a balance bar with a mark for the strength it takes to carry the position. Click your corps to send them in or hold them back, then auto-resolve or withdraw. The result comes back on the same screen with each corps's losses and what became of it. Fighting the battle yourself is the 3D build's job; the button is there and switched off.
- The province. A selected region shows what it holds as four slots (industry, works, harbour, airfields). On your own home ground the works slot is a button that builds the next level of fortification, and a row of cards raises new corps on the spot; whatever is forming there is listed under them. The rules are the war economy's own: this is the Build tab's orders given where the troops will appear.
- The other nations' month, watched. When the player ends the month the map travels to each nation that takes ground or fights a battle, says in a line what it did, and moves on; a Skip button plays the rest at once, and the opening screen has a switch to turn the watching off. The dispatches that follow put declarations of war, fallen capitals and capitulations on cards of their own, above the rest of the news.
- Campaign goals. Every nation a person can play has a short campaign and a long one (`data/campaign/goals.json`): cities to hold, nations to beat, and a date. Germany's short campaign is what history's Germany did, Warsaw, Oslo and Paris by the end of 1940. Poland's goals are counted in weeks, four to a month: the short one is to be in the war still when October opens (history's Warsaw surrendered on 28 September), the long one is to be fighting in the sixth week, longer than history's five, and it is won even if Poland goes down later. The goals are shown on the nation-pick screen for the September 1939 starts, ticked off on the opening screen as the war goes on, and announced in the dispatches when they are won or lost. They judge the war and never change it: no computer nation knows about them. A campaign that opens later leaves out goals whose date has gone by.
- Alerts and keys. A column of alerts on the map says what wants attention this month (armies at the front that have not moved, armies cut off from supply, a treasury in debt, money unspent, generals with skill points, an idle design team), and a click on one goes there. Enter ends the month, Escape backs out of whatever is open, and Space skips the watching.
- Stand, dig in or fall back. Every army of yours carries a standing order for the day the enemy comes, set with three buttons in the army tray. An army told to stand fights where it is. An army told to dig in entrenches: once it has sat a whole month on the same ground without marching or fighting it defends 25 percent stronger, the Battle Ticket says so, and it loses the trenches the moment it leaves them. An army told to fall back gives ground when the attack arrives with odds of 1.2 to 1 or better: it slips away whole to the ground behind it, loses some cohesion and the region, and no battle is fought. It does this once a month (caught again, it stands), fortress troops always stand, and nobody gives up their own capital this way. The ground given up is filed with the battles as "Fell back". The orders are the player's alone: no computer nation uses them, so history's wars are unchanged.
- Friend, enemy or neutral. Click any territory and a banner under its name says where the nation holding it stands with you: Yours, Ally (green), Enemy (red) or Neutral (grey), with a line that says why ("Germany is at war with you. Also at war with France.", "Italy is a member of the Axis, but is not at war with anyone.", "Hungary is neutral, and leaning toward the Axis."). An enemy is a nation you are at war with; an ally is a nation on your side of the war; everybody else is neutral, whatever bloc they sit in. On occupied ground the banner also says whose ground it is and where they stand. The same word rides on the label under the cursor, and it changes the month the war does. With nobody at the controls the banner names the bloc instead (Axis, Allies or Neutral). The rule is `standing` in `src/campaign/diplomacy.ts`.
- Supply traced from each nation's own capital, industry and depots. Armies that are cut off fight at half strength and waste away.
- Battles settled at once from a Battle Ticket (forces, terrain, forts, supply, air, commanders, tempo, direction of attack) with the framework's victory levels. Fortifications only count against the direction they face, so the Maginot Line can be outflanked.
- Nations ask for terms when they have lost too much of their capital, territory and army. France's fall creates Vichy; eastern Poland goes to the Soviet Union under the 1939 pact; sea-supplied armies can be lifted off from a port.
- Computer opponents that follow history's plan as data (`data/campaign/plan-historical.json`). The plan sets intentions only; every region still has to be won.
- A war economy. Factories pay into the treasury every month (half speed at peace, half value on occupied ground). Losses are made good first; what is left buys new infantry, mountain, motorized and armoured corps and fortifications, which take months to arrive. Computer-run nations start ordering once their scripted mobilisation is over. All costs are data (`data/campaign/production.json`). Money is shown at a hundred to the production point, so an infantry corps costs 4,800.
- The budget, in the grand campaign. Every province pays something to whoever holds it: a factory 100, a city 30, farmland 18, forest 12, hills 10, marsh and mountain 6, desert 3, a harbour 12 more, an oilfield 150, the capital 200. Conquered ground and colonies pay half. Every corps, ship and aircraft draws upkeep from the treasury each month (an infantry division 4, an armoured division 10, a battleship 8, a hundred bombers 10), halved for a nation at peace. Income less upkeep is what is left for replacements and new orders. A treasury that runs dry goes into debt: no replacements go out, nothing can be ordered, unpaid corps rest badly and lose men, and creditors stop lending at three months of income. Computer-run nations order nothing they could not keep in the field. `ledger(g, nat)` in `src/campaign/economy.ts` returns the whole of a nation's books for either interface to show; the numbers are in `data/campaign/budget.json`. The historical scenarios keep the plain arithmetic of factories, so that history's timetable holds.
- The ledger. The treasury is always on the top bar with what the month will add or take; a click opens the ledger: what is in hand, income, upkeep and what is left, one bar showing how much of the income the army, navy and air force each take, money in by source, money out by arm, and every province held with what it pays, richest first. A selected province shows what it is worth to its holder and what it would pay in the player's hands.
- The arsenal, in the grand campaign. The treasury buys more than corps. The Build tab has four shelves. **Army**: infantry, mountain troops, artillery, motorized troops and tanks. **Air force**: fighters and bombers by the wing of a hundred. **Navy**: destroyer and submarine flotillas, cruisers, carriers, battleships, and troopships that add to the sea lift. **Works**: factories, shipyards, anti-aircraft guns, airfields and fortifications, built in the province picked on the map. Every offer shows its price, how long it takes, what it costs to keep, where it would go and, when it cannot be ordered, why not. A selected province shows the same things as slots and cards: what can be raised, stationed, laid down or built right there.
- Heavy equipment needs industry. Infantry and mountain troops can be raised in any home province; motorized troops and artillery need a factory in the province, tanks two. Aircraft need an aircraft industry (a home province with two factories for fighters, three for bombers) and airfields to be stationed on: a province where one of the nation's air fleets is based, or one where airfields have been built, in which case the first wing founds a new air fleet there. Ships are laid down in a home port whose shipyard is big enough: level 1 for destroyers, submarines and troopships, level 2 for cruisers, level 3 for carriers and battleships, which take about two years. History's shipyards are on the map (`yards` in `data/campaign/arsenal.json`) and a yard can be enlarged.
- Factories are built in cities (a province that is urban, the capital, already has industry or is a place of some size), at most three new ones in a city and two in a town, each dearer than the last. A factory adds a production point to the income every month and counts toward what the province can raise.
- Artillery is a corps of massed guns. In the attack each artillery division takes 15% off the worth of the defender's fortifications and trenches, up to 60%; in the defence each adds 5% to the other defenders, up to 20%. It is slow and weak if caught alone. The Battle Ticket has a line for it.
- Anti-aircraft guns, levels 1 to 3, ring a province's industry: each level stops 15% of any bombing of that province and brings down more of the bombers, and the Battle Ticket of a raid says so. Airfields, levels 1 to 3, let a new air fleet be founded in a province for fighter patrols, and each level lets 500 more aircraft fly from it. Both cost a little every month to man, and the ledger lists them. A computer-run nation puts guns on its richest factory province once it has been bombed. These are campaign rules: the month's air campaigns are still settled from a Battle Ticket.
- The war at sea. Eighteen fleets start the war in their 1939 bases, each a count of battleships, carriers, cruisers, destroyers and submarines under a named admiral. A fleet patrols a sea to hold it, raids shipping, or lies in port to refit. Fleets that meet fight a fleet action settled from its own Battle Ticket, and aircraft flying from the coast count toward holding a sea, so the narrow waters belong to whoever owns the airfields. The naval rules are data (`data/campaign/navy.json`, `data/campaign/seas.json`).
- The war in the air. Twenty-two air fleets, each a count of fighters and bombers at the airfields of one region under a named commander: the Luftflotten, Fighter Command's groups, Bomber Command, the French air zones and the Soviet district air forces. Left alone a fleet supports the armies and defends the sky within reach of its fighters. Given a mission it fights a month-long air campaign, settled from its own Battle Ticket: win the sky over a region, bomb its industry by day or by night, or strike at shipping in a sea. The air rules are data (`data/campaign/air.json`).
- Range, radar and airfields. Fighters are at full effect to 60% of their range and useless beyond it, so bombers sent further go alone and suffer. Radar gets every defending fighter up in time; a defender without it meets raids with 70% of his fighters. The airfields of one region serve about 1,500 aircraft, and fleets packed in beyond that cannot all fly. Moving base costs readiness, and so does every month of operations.
- Aircraft and pilots. Factories and flying schools deliver on history's schedule into a reserve that tops the fleets up each month, resting fleets first. A new fighter needs a pilot. A pilot shot down over his own ground usually flies again; one shot down over the enemy's does not.
- The sky. An attacker wins the sky over a region when the fighters he has left at the end of the month outweigh, by 1.65 to 1, what the defender can still put up once his losses are made good. It lasts a month. An army attacking there has the air on its side, and no invasion fleet can cross to a coast while enemy fighters still hold the sky over it.
- Bombing cuts the victim's next month of production. Strikes on shipping sink warships at sea and coastal trade.
- The Battle of Britain. On history's plan the Luftwaffe fights over the Channel in July 1940, goes for the airfields of Kent and Hampshire in August, turns on London in September and goes over to the night Blitz in the autumn.
- Sea transport that can be intercepted. Troopships need sea lift and a route through seas the enemy has not closed. A crossing through contested water costs men, and an army overseas is supplied only while a port and an open sea link it to home.
- Landings from the sea. Corps in a port can be put ashore on an enemy coast up to three seas away. Troops storming a defended beach fight at 60% strength, unless they land in the very month war is declared, when surprise makes up the difference. Enemy fleets in harbour nearby put to sea against an invasion, and a landing cannot be attempted at all under a sky the enemy's fighters still hold. That is what makes Operation Sea Lion wait on the air battle.
- Norway, April 1940. Germany declares war on Denmark and Norway and lands at Oslo, Bergen, Trondheim and Narvik under cover of its Battle Fleet; the Home Fleet comes out to meet it and an Allied expedition goes ashore, then leaves when France is in danger.
- The convoy war. German submarines and raiders sink shipping on the Atlantic routes every month, and every ton lost cuts British production. Escorts, patrolling fleets and air cover cut the losses and sink U-boats. The U-boat arm grows on history's schedule.
- Swedish iron ore reaches Germany across the Baltic in summer and through Narvik when the Gulf of Bothnia freezes. In the winter months it arrives only while Narvik is in hands that are not at war with Germany, and losing it costs German industry.
- The war overseas. North Africa, the Levant and the island bases are colonies, and the rules treat them that way. A nation stands or falls by its home country, so losing Libya does not bring Italy nearer to asking for terms, and troops overseas are not counted in the army that defends the homeland. New corps are raised at home and sent out; history's expeditions (the Australians, the Afrika Korps, the Eighth Army's two corps) arrive in the month they did, at a friendly port, and only if the war they were sent for is being fought. When France falls the Army of Africa stays at its posts under Vichy. Battles overseas roll their own dice, so a fight in the desert never changes the luck of the war in Europe.
- The desert. There is one road, no water and nothing to live on, so an army in North Africa is only as strong as the port behind it. Every corps' supply line is counted from the nearest working port, a step for every region of desert: Tripoli and Alexandria are great harbours, Benghazi and Tobruk small ones that start as if two regions out. The longer the line, the weaker the attack, the thinner the defence, the slower the replacements and the shorter each bound forward into enemy ground. An ally's port feeds the corps who came to fight beside him. A fortified port keeps two months of stores after the sea behind it is closed, which is what lets Tobruk stand a siege. No railway crosses the desert. All of it is data (`data/campaign/desert.json`).
- Open desert. Infantry on foot outside prepared works has no flank to anchor on: against an attack that includes tanks or lorried troops it defends at 65%, and when it is driven off it loses more men than troops who can drive away. The Battle Ticket says so.
- The Libya convoys. Every man, tank and litre of fuel for the Axis army in Africa crosses the Central Mediterranean. Malta hunts those convoys for as long as it holds out, harder while it is itself supplied, and every tenth of the cargo sunk makes the Axis supply line in Libya half a region longer. With Malta in Axis hands the route is safe.
- The desert war on history's plan (`data/campaign/theatres.json`). An army overseas is run by a plan of its own, separate from its nation's plan in Europe: the Italians cross into Egypt in September 1940 and stop at Sidi Barrani, Operation Compass throws them back across Cyrenaica that winter, Rommel lands at Tripoli in February 1941 and wins it all back in the spring while Tobruk holds out behind him, and Operation Crusader lifts the siege at the end of the year.
- The desert in 1942. The Axis army beaten in Operation Crusader no longer stands and dies: it marches out of Cyrenaica to El Agheila in December 1941. In January Rommel comes back to Agedabia and Benghazi and the line settles at Gazala, where both armies build up through the spring. In May he turns the line at Bir Hakeim, in June Tobruk falls and the British go back to El Alamein, the last position before Alexandria. The Axis tries the Alamein line in July and is thrown back. Montgomery lands in August with a fresh armoured corps, the armour is brought forward in September while the Axis digs in behind minefields, and in October the British break the line and chase what is left of the Axis army back across Libya: Tobruk and Benghazi in November, El Agheila by the end of the year, with Tripoli still to take.
- A plan that depends on how the war has gone. In a side theatre several phases of a plan may cover the same months, and a phase can be made to wait until the enemy holds a named place (`ifLost`); the first phase in the list that fits is the one followed. The Axis leaves Cyrenaica only once the frontier is lost, the British leave Gazala for El Alamein the month Bir Hakeim or Tobruk falls, and the Axis leaves its line before El Alamein only once that line is broken.
- Falling back on a base. Each army in a theatre has a port it falls back on (`bases`): told to give ground up, its corps march toward it, and where the one road is full of its own troops those in front go a stage further so that those behind can get out. An army overseas has no railway, so when its plan gathers the armour the tanks march up from as far as a month's marching reaches, and infantry makes room for them.
- Works that change with the month (`works` in `data/campaign/desert.json`). The mines and wire of Tobruk were lifted to build the Gazala line, so from May 1942 it is a fortress of level 1, not 2; the Axis digs in before El Alamein in September. Each happens only if the place is still in the hands that did it, and the newsreel says so.
- The United States. It stands aside until December 1941, when Pearl Harbor brings it into the war against Germany and Italy. It is a power off the map: it has no regions and no capital, its ships come in from the North Atlantic, its industry counts though no region holds it, and it raises nothing on the map. Its army is the one history sent: II Corps under Fredendall and the Western Task Force under Patton sail for Torch in November 1942, and more divisions follow. Having no airfields on the map of its own, its air force flies only for its own army.
- Torch and Tunisia on history's plan. In November 1942 Britain and the United States go to war with Vichy France and land in Morocco and Algeria from Gibraltar. The French fight, and when December opens Darlan's ceasefire brings North Africa and the Levant over to the Allies, while Germany and Italy occupy the rest of France and Corsica and fly troops into Tunis. The British dash for Tunis in December is beaten off. Tripoli falls in January and the Axis army goes back past it to the Mareth Line. In February two panzer divisions strike the Americans in front of Tebessa and are thrown back. The Mareth Line is broken in March, Sfax falls in April and Tunis in May, where the Axis army in Africa lays down its arms. Anderson, Fredendall, Patton, von Arnim and Messe are named at the head of their formations.
- Events that wake a nation or divide one (`data/campaign/diplomacy.json`). An event can bring in a country that has stood aside (`wake`), and can end one and share out what it held (`partition`): each share goes to the first of its takers still in the war. Events can wait on a war between two nations, on a nation still standing aside, and on one or all of a list of places being in a bloc's hands. That is how Pearl Harbor, Darlan's ceasefire and the news that Africa is clear are written.
- More orders for an army overseas. A phase of a theatre's plan can name the place its army falls back on for the months it runs (`base`), which is how the Axis goes back to Mareth in January and into the Tunis bridgehead in the spring. The force a plan keeps at a place joins no attack elsewhere, so the garrison of the Mareth Line stays out of the stroke at Kasserine. A landing can carry other corps from the same quay in one lift (`with`), and tries each beach on its list in turn, passing over one it cannot storm at the plan's odds.
- Older saved games still load. A country added to the game since the save was written takes its place in the order of play, and the month goes on with whoever was moving. A place cut out of the map since (Cassino from the Abruzzo, Anzio from Rome) starts in the hands the ground it was cut from is in, with the same works, so a campaign saved on the map of 373 regions carries on. A save from the map before North Africa was added still cannot be continued.
- Side theatres. A theatre is a list of regions with plans of its own (`data/campaign/theatres.json`), and it can be on the mainland as well as overseas. A nation with a plan there runs every corps it has on that ground by that plan and the rest of its army by its main plan; neither army takes orders meant for the other, neither marches onto the other's ground, and battles in a theatre roll their own dice and their own generals' luck. Formations can be lent to a theatre for a set time and go back to the main army when it is up, so the panzer divisions that overrun Yugoslavia are in Poland again for Barbarossa.
- Greece and the Balkans on history's plan. Italy attacks out of Albania in October 1940 and is stopped in the mountains of Epirus all winter, the spring offensive included. Britain garrisons Crete and sends an expedition to Thessaly. In April 1941 officers in Belgrade overthrow the regent, Germany comes through Bulgaria, Romania and Hungary, and Yugoslavia and Greece fall within weeks. The occupation troops stay; the armour leaves.
- Havens. Troops lifted off a coast in a theatre are taken to that theatre's haven while it is in friendly hands and has room, not all the way home: the British expedition leaves Greece for Crete, and for Egypt only when Crete goes too. An expedition's plan can tell it to get out once a named place has fallen.
- Airborne troops. An airborne corps takes off from a friendly airfield and comes down on any enemy coast across one sea, with no ships and no regard for who holds the water, unless the enemy holds the sky over it. It fights without its heavy weapons, at 90%, and the transport aircraft can carry one such assault a month. Crete falls this way in May 1941, at a price.
- Shared shipping. In a side theatre a port that any ally's ships can reach feeds the troops who hold it, so a German garrison on a Greek island lives on Italian convoys.
- Saved games. The whole campaign, including the exact state of the random number stream, is written to the browser as you play, and the start screen offers to continue it. A loaded game plays out exactly as the unsaved one would have.
- Diplomacy on the framework's seven-step alignment track. Every neutral has a public opinion that drifts back to its own leaning and bends toward whoever is winning the war. A country that leans toward a bloc trades with it; one that is fully won over joins it. Players spend political capital to court neutrals, and moves that match public opinion go further.
- History as events (`data/campaign/diplomacy.json`): the Soviet occupation of the Baltic states and Bessarabia, the Vienna Award, the Tripartite Pact. Each fires once, in its window, and only if its conditions still hold. If France does not fall, the Danube states do not join the Axis.
- The eastern front of 1941. Germany's plan runs from the build-up in Poland through Barbarossa, Smolensk, Kiev and the drive on Moscow to the winter line; Romania marches with it, and Finland, Hungary and Slovakia declare war. The Soviet plan holds, then strikes back in December.
- Supply that thins out with distance. Inside the Soviet Union every army's supply line is measured from its railheads: the longer it is, the weaker its attacks, the thinner its defence, the slower its replacements and the more each step forward costs. Captured railways are converted over a few months. All of it is data (`data/campaign/east.json`).
- Mud and winter. Mud in November and April slows every army in the east; each season is counted from the month after it sets in, so March is still frozen ground that carries an army. From December to February armies without winter equipment fight and march badly on Soviet ground and lose men to the cold. The Red Army and the Finns do not.
- The Red Army's mobilisation. From the month it is attacked the Soviet Union raises new rifle corps every month, and defence lines are dug around Leningrad and then Moscow.
- The eastern front of 1942 and the winter after it. Germany takes its tanks out of the quiet parts of the line in the spring and gathers them in the south. Case Blue opens in June: Voronezh, then the Don and Rostov in July, the land bridge to the Volga in August and the Caucasus as far as Maikop and the Terek by the autumn. Stalingrad is assaulted across the land bridge and holds. In November the Red Army, which has stood quiet all summer, breaks the Romanian armies on both flanks and closes the ring; the army on the Volga starves and lays down its arms in January. The German army in the Caucasus gets out to the Kuban coast in time. The Soviet winter offensive takes Voronezh and Kursk; in February Germany gives up Rostov once the Caucasus army is through and holds the Mius in front of Stalino, and in March the German counter-stroke retakes Kharkov before the thaw stops everything. An army told to leave a salient makes room on the road for the corps behind it, so nobody is left at the far end because the next stage was full.
- Kursk and the road to the Dnieper, 1943. The spring is quiet. In June the Red Army digs three belts of defences round the Kursk salient and forms the Central and Voronezh Fronts and two tank armies inside it, with the Steppe Front and three more tank armies behind; Rokossovsky, Vatutin, Katukov, Rotmistrov and Rybalko are named at their head. Germany gathers its tanks at Orel and Kharkov and in July attacks the salient once, and is held. The fronts in the salient are then let loose on Orel, the reserves take Kharkov in August, and in September the German army gives up everything east of the Dnieper and mans the river from Kiev to Zaporozhye, while the Red Army takes Smolensk, Bryansk and the Donbas behind it. The Red Army is over the river in October and in Kiev in November or December. If it takes Zhitomir too, the panzers strike back there before the year is out.
- A plan can allow one assault and no more. Germany's plan for Kursk attacks the salient once; the army is not sent in a second time the same month, as it would be anywhere else.
- Sicily and Italy, 1943. Italy is a theatre of its own, with its own plan and its own dice. In June an Allied air command under Tedder forms at Malta and wins the sky over Sicily, because no invasion fleet can cross under enemy fighters. In July the Eighth Army under Montgomery and Leese lands in Sicily from Malta with the battle fleet covering it, and the Seventh Army under Patton and Bradley follows it ashore: twelve divisions, taken out of the armies in Africa. Guzzoni's Italians are driven across the strait, where Hube's panzer corps is waiting. Mussolini falls as August opens. As September opens Italy signs an armistice and leaves the war with its army, fleet and air force: Germany takes over Rome, the north and the Italian garrisons abroad, and the Allies get Sardinia and Corsica. That month the Germans go back to the mountains south of Rome and leave a rearguard at Naples, the Eighth Army crosses the strait and lands in the heel, and the Fifth Army under Clark lands at Salerno and takes Naples. The air command moves up to Foggia. In October German engineers dig the Gustav Line, and it holds to the end of the year with Rome behind it.
- Italy, 1944: Anzio, Cassino, Rome and the Gothic Line. The Gustav Line has two ends on the map now: Cassino, the mountain above the one good road from Naples to Rome, and the Abruzzo behind it, with the small port of Anzio on the coast between Cassino and Rome. In January Eisenhower, Montgomery, Bradley and Patton leave for England with seven veteran divisions, and the two armies in Italy are rebuilt round what is left: the Eighth under Leese, with the Canadians, and the Fifth under Clark, with Juin's French mountain troops. That month VI Corps lands at Anzio against nobody and digs in, and the first attack on Cassino fails. In February Mackensen's 14th Army tries to throw the beachhead into the sea (Operation Fischfang) and cannot, and the New Zealanders fail at Cassino; in March they fail again. In April nobody attacks: the Eighth Army comes across the mountains, Anders brings the Polish II Corps into the line, two new American divisions arrive, and the air forces win the sky over the line. In May, Operation Diadem breaks it: the British and Polish assaults wear the defenders down and the Fifth Army goes through. The Germans give up Rome without a battle in June and fall back to Tuscany and the Marche, the Allies take both in August, and the Germans stand on the Gothic Line, dug across the northern Apennines in July. In August VI Corps and the French leave for the landing in the south of France. The Eighth Army attacks the Gothic Line in September and the Fifth in October, both fail, and the front sits in the mountains for the winter.
- The West, 1944: Overlord to the Ardennes. France, the Low Countries and the German frontier become a theatre of their own in May 1944, with their own plan and their own dice; until then the ground belongs to the main fronts, as it did in 1940. That month the Atlantic Wall is manned from Calais to Normandy, two corps of the Second Army under Dempsey and Montgomery gather in Hampshire and two of the First Army under Bradley and Collins in Cornwall, and two tactical air forces win the sky over the beaches. In June the British and Canadians land in Normandy and the Americans on the Cotentin, and two harbours are built off the open beach. In July the British attack toward the Seine fails against five German corps (Goodwood) and the Americans break out at Rennes (Cobra). In August the German armour goes west for a counter-stroke, the British break the line it left, Patton's Third Army goes through Maine to Orleans and Paris, and VI Corps and the French land at Marseille from Naples. September is the pursuit: Calais, Brussels, Antwerp, Liege, Luxembourg, Lyon. Germany re-arms the West Wall that month and mans it with armies put together from what came out of France: Student's parachute army on the Dutch rivers, the Seventh Army at Aachen and in the Eifel, the First at Metz, the Nineteenth in the Vosges. In October Market Garden fails at Arnhem and the first attack on the wall fails at Aachen. November takes Metz, Strasbourg and Belfort and fails in the Hurtgen Forest. In December two panzer armies come out of the Eifel and take the Ardennes, and in January the Americans take them back.
- What the West needed from the rules. A theatre can open in its own month (`opens`), and in free play never does. Works can face a named neighbour (`vs`), so the Atlantic Wall stops a landing from England and not an army already ashore, and can bring a harbour with them (`port`), which a saved game remembers. One air fleet can have fighters with a reach of their own (`ftrRange`). A formation can wait on a defeat (`ifLost`): the armies of the West Wall form only once Paris is lost, and until then the corps they would be made from fight on. Allies in a theatre can draw a boundary between them (`entrust`), and each closes up to its own front. A plan can leave a garrison for others to watch (`mask`): nobody attacks the Atlantic pockets, closes up to them or stands guard over them. And an event can hand back ground an occupier has walked away from (`liberate`): in October the empty south and centre of France pass to the Allies, and in December the pockets do.
- Formations that change. A row of history's order of battle can now send divisions into a corps already at the front (`joins`: the corps keeps its general and what it has learned, and takes the new name and kind), form a corps by breaking up others (`from` with a list of names: they are struck off as it forms), and lend a formation to a campaign for a time (`until`: it leaves that month, and the dispatches say so). A beachhead digs in: works marked `taken` in `theatres.json` go up as the month closes, for an army that holds the place then, so the enemy's counter-attack meets them. A plan's retreat can take the garrisons with it (`release`). The main army is no longer railed into a side theatre. And an event can be news of the month just ended (`past`): the fall of Cassino and of Rome are dated to the month they happened in.
- The armies of 1944 (`data/campaign/diplomacy.json`). In March 1944 the Luftwaffe leaves the front for the defence of the Reich (its support for the armies falls from 100 to 45). In May the American army is re-rated from 0.9 to 1.0 and the Allied air forces from 70 and 60 to 140 and 90, and the German army from 1.25 to 1.15. All three wait for the wars that caused them: none happens to a Germany that is not at war with America or the Soviet Union.
- The eastern front, 1944: Leningrad to Budapest. The Red Army's plan names each month's objectives and nothing else. January lifts the siege of Leningrad at Novgorod and takes Kirovograd. February takes Rovno and Lutsk (Volhynia), and Army Group North digs in on the Panther line at Pskov and on the Narva. March is the mud offensive: Uman, Vinnitsa, Nikolaev and the Carpathian foothills. April takes Odessa and May the Crimea, where the Seventeenth Army is lost. In June the blow falls on Finland at Vyborg (Karelia) and on Army Group Centre at Vitebsk and Minsk; in July the army group is destroyed at Mogilev, with seventy to a hundred and thirty thousand prisoners, and the front goes to Wilno, Brest, Lublin and Lwow. In August the Red Army reaches the Vistula and stops there, the Polish 1st Army forms behind it, Warsaw rises, and the attack at Jassy brings Romania over: King Michael's coup hands the country and the Ploiesti oil to the Red Army, and two Romanian armies march with it. In September Bulgaria follows without a shot, Finland signs an armistice that gives up Karelia and Petsamo and leaves the country standing, the German Sixth Army is lost for the second time in Bessarabia, and Estonia is given up. October takes Riga, Debrecen and Belgrade and shuts Army Group North into Courland, where it is fed by sea to the end. Horthy's armistice is overthrown, Budapest is fortified, the first assault on it fails in November, and the year ends with the city ringed and the front on the Vistula. The German plan answers as Hitler ordered: fortified places held to the last (Vitebsk, Mogilev), ground given up only once it was already lost, and the reserves gathered at Warsaw, in East Prussia, in Courland and at Budapest.
- What the east needed from the rules. A plan can keep its army `strict`: it attacks only its `targets`, steps into no empty ground beyond them and makes no counter-attack, which is what keeps a front of two thousand kilometres to history's calendar. It may still walk into an island of ground the enemy has left altogether, and against a player it is not bound: ground a human enemy holds is stepped into and won back as freely as by any other army. An event can make `peace` between two nations inside the bigger war (each goes home), a `cede` signs over ground the taker already holds, and a `partition` marked `occupied` leaves each piece of the country to whoever has troops standing in it. A formation an event raises forms at the nearest place behind a front too crowded to form on. Dated works on the main front (`data/campaign/east.json`, `works`) dig the Panther line, the Courland harbour and Budapest's Attila line, in history only. Germany's replacements are shared between the fronts, so the army in Italy mends more slowly while Bagration is on, and the Fifth Army's July attack toward the Arno now waits for better odds (`minOdds` 1.6) so that Tuscany and the Marche still fall together in August.
- Landing ships. Troops fighting their way off a beach count for 60 percent of their strength. From June 1943 British and American troops count for 80 percent, for the landing ships, amphibious lorries and naval gunfire they have by then. Who has them, and from when, is data (`data/campaign/navy.json`).
- Armies made from armies. A formation can be put together out of an army already overseas: the invasion force for Sicily takes its divisions out of the British and American armies in Africa, and they shrink by as much. A corps left with no divisions is struck off, and garrisons are never drawn on.
- The land bridge. Between the bend of the Don and the Volga there is one region, Kalach, and it is the only way to Stalingrad from the west. The Don flank above it and the Kalmyk steppe below it are separate regions, so an army on the land bridge is cut off the moment both go.
- Orders a plan can give (`data/campaign/plan-historical.json`). Besides where to attack and at what odds, a month of history's plan can now say: gather so many tank and lorried corps at a place by rail (`concentrate`), keep so many corps massed at a place whatever else happens (`staging`), leave a stretch of the line to an ally and take your own corps out once he is there (`entrust`), pay a price of its own for one prize (`oddsFor`, which is how Stalingrad is assaulted at odds the plan would refuse anywhere else), stand quiet and attack nothing (`quiet`), and give ground up and march out before the road home is cut (`abandon`). The same orders can be given to an army in a side theatre. The side panel explains each one in plain words to a player whose nation has such an order that month, at home or overseas, and the player is free to ignore it.
- Germany's allies on the Don. The Hungarian 2nd Army, the Italian 8th Army and the Romanian 3rd and 4th Armies arrive through the summer of 1942 and take over the Don front from Voronezh to the Kalmyk steppe, so that the German corps can go on to the Volga. They go in beside whoever stands there, however crowded the place, and what is left of the Hungarians and Italians goes home in the spring of 1943. Italy follows Germany into the war in the east by an event, and only into a war Germany has actually begun.
- News of an encirclement. The newsreel reports the month a ring closes around three corps or more, with the men cut off, and reports the end as one piece of news when they lay down their arms.
- Limited manpower. However rich the treasury, an army gets back only about three men in a hundred of its peak strength each month, shared along the front.
- Staffs that learn. The Red Army of 1941 can bring only eight corps into one battle and does not renew a failed assault in the same month. In June 1943 an event remakes it: its troops are better, its air force is the equal of Germany's, it can bring fourteen corps into a battle, renews its attacks and moves reserves by rail like any other army. An event of this kind can remake any nation's army, and it is written in the data: another takes the heart out of the Italian army in July 1943, once Tunis is lost, and its divisions fight at three fifths of what they were worth. Defenders who hold their ground lose fewer men than those who are driven off it.
- Computer generals who bring reserves up behind a spearhead, garrison what they have taken, leave a besieged city alone when the plan says to starve it out, counter-attack ground they have just lost, and close any gap in a line they are standing on.
- Generals. The real commanders of 1939 start the war at the head of their corps, and others wait for a command. A general gains experience from every battle, and each level brings a skill point for one of four skills: offense, defense, logistics and leadership. Traits are earned from how he actually fights: three victories that end in surrender make an encirclement master, three positions held against a stronger attacker make an iron defender, three costly failed assaults make a butcher. Generals can be wounded, captured or killed, and unknown officers make their names in battle and rise from the ranks. The player assigns, relieves, promotes and dismisses them and spends their skill points; the computer does the same for its own. Everything a general adds is shown on the Battle Ticket. All of it is data (`data/campaign/commanders.json`).
- Unit stories. Every corps, air fleet and fleet keeps a service record (battles, breakthroughs, stands against the odds, prisoners, missions flown, tonnage sunk) and a diary, and one that has done enough is honoured. There are 22 honours in `data/campaign/stories.json`, each with a ribbon, a citation and the real precedent it is modelled on, from a capital on the colours to a full tour of twenty-five bombing missions, and an honoured unit may be given a name by its army. The player decides what to do with the fame: carry the honour at the front, where it makes the unit fight a few per cent harder (12% at most for one unit), or send its heroes home, on a bond tour for production points and political capital, or to the schools as instructors so that every later draft and new corps is better trained. A corps that goes home is out of the line for two months. Experience has names (green, trained, seasoned, veteran, elite); green drafts thin out what a corps of the player's knows, and a corps can be told to take none and stay small. A corps defends the region it was raised in harder and loses heart when it falls. One that keeps meeting the same enemy corps makes an old enemy of it, fights harder against it, and is rewarded for destroying it. Destroyed corps go on a memorial wall with their records and can be raised again under the old name, with the old colours and part of what they knew. Up to two letters a month from the player's own units come with the dispatches, and generals hold their army's ranks and are promoted in the newsreel. Records, honours and the wall are kept for every nation, but everything that changes a number applies to the player's own units only, and none of it draws on the dice, so a war the computer plays comes out exactly as it did before (checked for twelve wars to January 1945).
  - For the Unreal build, everything a screen needs is in the saved game. Each unit carries `rec` (its counters), `log` (diary lines as `[turn, what, where, n]`), `hon` (ids on the roll of honour) and `nick`. `stories` holds `awards` (the roll), `fallen` (the wall), `rivals` and `schools`. Honour names, ribbon colours, citations, thresholds, edges and every letter are data in `stories.json`, whose `_about` block explains each key. The player's choices are three orders in `turn.ts`: `orderCash`, `orderDrafts`, and `orderBuildCorps` with a formation from the wall.
- Research and doctrine. The five great powers each have eight design teams, one to a branch: tanks, infantry weapons and guns, army doctrine, fighters, bombers, the navy, radar and signals, and war production. Research runs on history's clock: a branch with one team gets every new design in the month history got it, from the 50 mm Panzer III and the Spitfire Mk V to the Panther, the Lancaster and the T-34/85. A second or third team brings a branch's designs forward, a branch with no team stands still, and extra teams can be founded with production points. What counts in battle is how far a branch is ahead of or behind its own history: each design in service early is worth 5 to 8 percent to the corps, fleets, fighters, bombers or factories that use it until history catches up, and each one missed costs the same. Doctrine is each army's own: Blitzkrieg, Deep Battle, Superior Firepower, Methodical Battle, War of Rapid Decision. The computer leaves one team on every branch, so its nations fight exactly as they did. The Research tab shows every branch as a strip of the whole war, with history's dates, the designers' progress and today marked on it. All of it is data (`data/campaign/research.json`).
- The eve of war. Germany moves first every month, and on history's plan Poland is beaten inside the first one. A player who takes Poland therefore places the army on 31 August 1939, before anyone moves: marches and rail moves on Polish ground, nothing across the frontier, and no month passes. The staff's advice is on the screen (`data/campaign/eve.json`), and one button places the army as the staff would: the strongest corps in Warsaw, the rest on the ground south of it, nobody in the east where the Red Army will cross. What happens next is measured over many wars and held by tests. Left strung along the frontier as it was, the army is destroyed and Poland is out in the fourth week, as in history. Placed as the staff advises, it holds Warsaw through September and falls in the fifth week. Placed so, with the Warsaw garrison told to dig in and the field armies told to fall back when they are outmatched, it is still fighting in the sixth week. Nothing holds Warsaw for good against the whole German army: the aim is to do better than history did.
- The grand campaign is the front door. The start screen asks for a country (Germany, the United Kingdom, France, the Soviet Union, Italy, Poland, or nobody, to watch) and a year: September 1939, or the first of January 1940, 1941 or 1942 (`data/campaign/grand.json`). History is played to that day and then put away: from the day you take over the war is free play, in which you and every computer-run nation's staff decide for yourselves. A country is offered only in the years history still has it in the war, so France can begin in 1939 or 1940 and Poland only in 1939. The years after 1942 are not offered: with nobody holding it to history's calendar the Red Army of 1943 ends the war inside a year, so those years are played as scenarios. The pick is remembered for next time.
- Historical scenarios, on the second menu, where the nations you do not play go on following history's plan. Play Germany, France, the United Kingdom or Poland from September 1939, Germany or the United Kingdom from July 1940 with the Battle of Britain about to begin, the United Kingdom or Italy from September 1940 with the Italian army on the Egyptian frontier, Greece or Italy from October 1940 with the Italian army about to cross the Greek frontier, Germany or the Soviet Union from June 1941, the United Kingdom or Italy from May 1942 with Rommel striking at the Gazala line, Germany or the Soviet Union from June 1942 with Case Blue about to open, the United Kingdom or Italy from November 1942 with the Torch convoys at Gibraltar, Germany or the Soviet Union from July 1943 with the panzers gathered on both sides of the Kursk salient, the United Kingdom or Italy from the same month with the invasion fleet gathering at Malta, the United Kingdom from January 1944 with the Fifth Army about to land at Anzio, Germany or the Soviet Union from the same month with the Red Army about to break the siege of Leningrad, the United Kingdom or Germany from May 1944 with the invasion armies on the quays in Hampshire, or Germany or the Soviet Union from June 1944 with Operation Bagration about to open. You can also watch history unfold from any of the twelve dates, from July 1943 with the map on either Kursk or Sicily, and from January 1944 on either Italy or the eastern front.
- Free play ("open history" in the framework). The war begins as it did on 1 September 1939, with Germany, Poland, Britain and France at war, and after that nobody follows history's plan. Every computer-run nation has a staff that weighs each of its fronts every month: its own attack against the enemy's defence near the line, with forts, terrain, season and air counted. It opens an offensive when the odds reach what that nation's temper asks for (Germany attacks at even odds, France wants two to one), keeps it up while it gains ground, calls it off when it stalls, and rests before it tries again. History's great decisions are written as choices a staff may take up when their conditions hold: Norway, the attack in the west through the Low Countries, the Balkans, the attack on the Soviet Union, Italy's entry and its Greek adventure, a Soviet first strike, and the small Axis allies following Germany east. Each takes months of build-up, has its season, and comes up or not by a seeded draw, and every nation's nerve differs a little from one war to the next. So Germany may go west in March 1940 or October 1941, Italy may stay out, the war in the east may start in 1941, in 1945, from the Soviet side, or never. The events of diplomacy still happen when their conditions come true, but none of them brings a country at peace into the war. Play Germany, the United Kingdom, France, Poland, Italy or the Soviet Union, or watch. Nobody hands the player a plan: the orders panel gives your own staff's reading of the odds on each front instead. All of it is data (`data/campaign/open.json`), and the same seed still replays the same war.

The British expedition is lifted off to Crete, and home when Crete falls to the airborne corps, which loses more than 2,000 men taking it. By the end of June no German tank is left in the Balkans, Barbarossa starts on time, and no corps of either German army has strayed onto the other's ground.
- Research moves that balance, and that is tested too. A Britain that put second teams on fighters and radar in 1939 keeps its sky even against all five air fleets. A Germany that put three teams on fighters in 1939 has the Bf 109F in the summer of 1940 and the Fw 190 by October, and wins the sky over Kent with the two western air fleets alone.
- "No two wars alike" (free play, twelve wars from September 1939 to the end of 1944 with no plan followed): Poland falls in the autumn of 1939 in every one. Germany attacks in the west in most, in at least four different months, and France falls where it is attacked in strength. Italy picks its own moment. War comes in the east in most wars, begun by either side and never in the first winter. The wars do not end the same way: Moscow and Berlin each stand in some and fall in others.

Where 1941 still differs from history: Odessa falls a month or two early, Rostov is never reached, Soviet losses are lower than the real ones, and Finland, Hungary and Slovakia declare war but only defend. In the desert, the Axis army gets away from Operation Crusader to El Agheila stronger than the real one did, with about 100,000 men. In the Balkans, Albania is one region, so the Greek counter-offensive that took Koritsa is not drawn: the front stays on the frontier. Crete falls in nearly every trial war, where the real battle was a close-run thing, and the troops sent to Greece are extra to the desert army rather than taken from it, so Rommel's first offensive is not helped by their going. Italy and Bulgaria gain nothing in April 1941, and there are no occupation zones: Germany holds all of Yugoslavia and Greece.

Where 1942 differs from history: the army that is lost is cut off on the land bridge west of Stalingrad, not in the city, which the Germans never enter; it is three to five corps and 100,000 to 270,000 men where the real pocket held about 250,000, and it is gone in January, not on 2 February. There is no relief attempt and no airlift. The Soviet offensive at Kharkov in May 1942 costs 60,000 to 70,000 men, not a quarter of a million. Kharkov changes hands in February and March in about a third of the trial wars and is never lost in the rest. Nothing is planned for the north and the centre in 1942, so there is no fighting at Rzhev or Demyansk and no attempt to lift the siege of Leningrad. In about one war in ten the German army of 1941 never reaches Kharkov or the Donets, and its 1942 offensive starts from further back.

Where the desert of 1942 differs from history: the map has one region between El Alamein and Sidi Barrani, so the Axis line of the autumn stands at Mersa Matruh and the October battle carries that name on its Battle Ticket. The Axis army at El Alamein is about 150,000 men where the real one had some 116,000, and it tries the line in July and seldom again, where history saw a second attempt at Alam Halfa at the end of August. There is no army level above the corps, so Montgomery leads the armoured corps he arrives with. Land battles still take their air support from each nation's air rating, so the Battle Tickets of October give the sky to the Axis, which is wrong. The desert generals of 1940 and 1941 other than Rommel, Cruwell, O'Connor and Montgomery are still unnamed.

Where Torch and Tunisia differ from history: Algiers is too strongly held to storm, so the British land further east, at Constantine, and Algiers comes over with the ceasefire, which arrives when December opens, not after three days. The Levant passes to Britain at the same ceasefire, because the Syrian campaign of 1941 is not in the game. The French who came over fight as one corps under the British flag. Rommel stays in Africa to the end and can be taken prisoner there. After Kasserine the Americans mostly hold their front, and Sfax and Tunis fall to the British: there are no separate battles of El Guettar or Bizerte. The stroke at Kasserine is made by two divisions, and the British take the ground back the same month. American neutrality and Lend-Lease before Pearl Harbor are not modelled, American air power counts only in battles the Americans fight themselves, and the United States cannot be played.

Where 1943 in the east differs from history: the two sides lose about the same number of men from July to December, where the Red Army really lost several times what Germany did, because under these rules the side that wins a battle loses less. Citadel is one battle for the whole salient, decided in a month, so there is no Prokhorovka and no separate northern and southern thrust. Orel falls in July, a month early, and to the fronts from inside the salient. In about half the wars the Red Army walks into the Dnieper bend at Kirovograd in the autumn, two or three months early, because Germany has no infantry left to post there. Kiev falls in November in most wars and in December in the rest. The German stroke at Zhitomir only happens in the wars where the Red Army has the town when December opens, about one in five, and the town changes hands twice inside the month. The Kuban bridgehead, the Mius and the Nevel battles are not fought as such. When Italy leaves the war in September, the share of its air force that counted for the Axis in the east goes with it, which is worth a few percent to the Red Army from then on.

Where Sicily and Italy differ from history: Sicily is one region, so the five weeks of fighting for the island are one battle on the beaches, decided in July; the German divisions that really fought on the Etna line and got away over the strait in August stand in Calabria from the start and are never in that battle. The same is true at Salerno: the Fifth Army meets one panzer division there and takes the whole Naples region in September, where the city really fell on 1 October, and the German counter-attack on the beachhead is not fought. The dice decide very little in this campaign: all sixty test wars ran to the same calendar. After Naples nothing more is fought in 1943, where the Allies really spent November and December forcing the Volturno, the Sangro and the Bernhardt Line at heavy cost. The Italian fleet is laid up in port and does not sail to Malta. There is no Italian Social Republic, no Italian army fighting beside the Allies, no rescue of Mussolini, and no fighting between Germans and Italians in Rome, the Balkans or the Dodecanese. Corsica goes to Britain with Sardinia, because the Free French are not a separate army. The Mediterranean Air Command is British in the game: the American air forces that were half of it are not counted separately.

Where Italy in 1944 differs from history: the dice decide very little here either, and all sixty test wars ran to the same calendar within a month. The four battles for Cassino are four dice throws at one region, so the bombing of the abbey, the Rapido crossings and the fighting in the town are not told apart, and the breakthrough in May is credited to the Fifth Army as a whole, where it was Juin's corps in the Aurunci mountains that turned the line. The beachhead at Anzio is one corps in one small region: it holds or it does not, and the long siege of February to May is a quiet front. The breakout from Anzio in May is not a battle of its own, and VI Corps reaches Rome in June by way of Cassino. Rome falls in June as it did, but with the German 10th Army getting away whole, there is no argument to be had about Clark turning for Rome. The pursuit is quicker on the coast and slower on the Adriatic than it was: Tuscany and the Marche both fall in August, where Ancona really fell in July and Florence in August. On the Gothic Line the Eighth Army really broke through to Rimini in September and the Fifth came within nine miles of Bologna in October; here both are plain defeats in front of Emilia, because the line and the plain behind it are one region. The Germans lose about 115,000 men in the year's battles to the Allies' 200,000, where the real figures were nearer equal: under these rules the side that fails an assault pays for it and the side that retreats pays little. The troops that leave for the south of France in August simply leave the map: Dragoon is not fought yet. There is no Italian co-belligerent army, no partisans behind the German lines and no bombing of the Brenner. The British X Corps under the Fifth Army is an American corps in the game, as it has been since Salerno, and leaves the map in April rather than moving to the Eighth Army.

Where the West in 1944 differs from history: the dice decide almost nothing, and all sixty test wars ran to the same calendar. Normandy is two regions, so the landing is one battle on each beach and the seven weeks of fighting in the hedgerows and round Caen are one failed British attack and one successful American one; the German counter-stroke at Mortain is a concentration of armour that is then beaten where it stands, and is never an attack of its own. The Allies come ashore more slowly than they did, with fourteen divisions in June and twenty-six in July. The British start September on the Seine and cannot reach the Dutch rivers until October, so Market Garden is a month late and is a plain assault from Brabant, with no airborne drop: the Airborne Army arrives in Brussels as infantry. Aachen is part of the Rhineland region, which holds, so the city's fall in October is news and not a change on the map. The Scheldt is not fought for: Antwerp is a working port from the day it is taken. The Atlantic fortresses are stormed in September (Brest, Calais) or starve by the winter (Bordeaux), where Lorient, Saint-Nazaire, La Rochelle and Dunkirk really held out until May 1945. All four of Lorraine, Alsace, the Vosges and Belfort are cleared in November, so there is no Colmar pocket and no fighting on the Saar in December. Liberated France is held by the American and British armies: France is not restored as a nation, and de Lattre's French corps fight under the American flag. The December offensive takes the whole Ardennes region in one battle and does not try for the Meuse or Antwerp; Bastogne is news. The German plan makes no local counter-attacks at all, and the Allied air forces fly no missions after June beyond supporting the armies. The Germans lose about 530,000 men in battle and the Allies about 260,000: roughly half of what the campaign really cost each side between June and January, prisoners included.

Where the East in 1944 differs from history: the dice decide almost nothing, and the twenty test wars ran to the same calendar within a month. Each offensive is one or two battles for a region, so Korsun, Kamenets-Podolsky and the Brody pocket are not told apart, and Bagration is three battles in June and one great surrender at Mogilev in July where it was a dozen encirclements; Minsk falls at the end of June, a few days early. The Red Army is too big and wins too cheaply: it puts nine to twelve million men on the map where it had six and a half million at the front, and it loses about half of what the Axis loses in battle, where in fact it lost far more men than it destroyed. The German army in the east is about 1.3 million men in January where it had over two million. The Warsaw rising and the Slovak rising are news and not battles, and Slovakia stays an Axis country to the end of the story. Romania's and Bulgaria's armies go over as three Soviet corps and their countries become Soviet-held ground: neither comes back as a nation, and nor does Yugoslavia when Belgrade is freed. Finland makes peace and keeps Helsinki, and the Lapland war against the Germans in the north is not fought. Army Group North is not attacked in Courland at all, where six battles failed to break it. Budapest is assaulted once in November and, in about half the wars, once more in December; the ring closes as news, and the city's fall in February 1945 is beyond the story's end. In a slow war a German pocket left over from 1943 at Tikhvin or Kalinin (three wars in twenty) is cleared in January 1944 with a hundred thousand prisoners or more.

Where free play stops short: the computer makes no landings from the sea of its own beyond the two Norway expeditions, so a computer-run Britain and America never invade North Africa, Italy or France, and Germany is only ever beaten from the east; a player can make those landings himself. The invasion forces history assembled for Sicily and Italy are not sent, since they belong to landings history planned. In twenty test wars to January 1946, eighteen saw war in the east: the Soviet Union was beaten in four, Germany in four, and ten were undecided. The computer's air fleets only support their armies, its research never changes, there is no negotiated peace, and the United States still enters in December 1941 whatever has happened in Europe.

**The flight model** for the air battle (`src/air/flight.ts`) reproduces the documented speed, climb and turn of the Spitfire, Hurricane, Bf 109, Bf 110, Do 17 and He 111 from their physical data, and is covered by tests.

## What is not here yet

- The gray-box air battle screen (the other half of Phase 1). The flight model, scenario data and pilot logic exist; the battle loop and the command screen do not.
- The rest of the war in the air. The computer flies air offensives only where history's plan has one, which today means Germany against Britain in 1940 and 1941, the Allies against Sicily in 1943, against the Gustav Line in the spring of 1944 and over the invasion beaches that May: it does not fly a British night offensive against Germany, does not react to a player's air offensive by moving its fighter groups, and never attempts Sea Lion itself. Support for the armies still comes from each nation's air rating, not from where its air fleets stand. There are no aircraft types and no carrier aircraft, and air transport is only the one airborne assault a month.
- The rest of research: the computer never changes its research priorities, there is no retooling cost when a new design enters production, no captured or Lend-Lease equipment, no stolen technology, and none of the framework's superweapon projects. The United States has no design teams and Japan is not in the game yet, so Carrier Warfare and Strategic Bombing are not yet doctrines of their own.
- The rest of the war at sea: building warships to order (today new ships arrive on history's schedule), carrier strikes and port raids, and mines. Sea battles are settled at once from fleet strength; there is no tactical naval battle.
- The rest of the Mediterranean: the fighting in Syria and Iraq, East Africa, the Malta convoys, and the partisan war in Yugoslavia and Greece. Malta's garrison is supplied like any other port and its bite on the Libya convoys is one number; the only air fleet based in the Mediterranean is the Allied one formed for Sicily in June 1943, so there is no air siege of the island, and the computer never tries to take it. The computer's fleets do not fight for the Libya route.
- The rest of diplomacy: changing your own nation's side, treaties, espionage, and computer-run nations that court neutrals themselves. Today the computer follows history's events and the drift of the war.
- Italy after 1944: the last winter on the Gothic Line and the spring offensive of 1945 are not planned yet. A computer-run Italy has no plan of its own for the campaign of 1943: it defends its country by the general rules until the armistice.
- The last winter, and 1945. History's plans run to January 1945 on every front: Italy to the Gothic Line, the West to the closing of the Bulge, the East to the siege of Budapest. The Vistula offensive, the crossing of the Rhine and the end in Germany are not planned yet, and from February 1945 the computer-run nations only hold their lines. A computer-run Germany facing a player in the east in 1944 stands on the defensive and makes no counter-strokes of its own. Free play has no such end.
- The rest of the arsenal: a computer-run nation buys only anti-aircraft guns from it (its fleets and air forces still grow on history's programmes, and it builds no factories), nothing bought can be disbanded or sold, and works do not show on the map itself, only in the province panel. There are no raw resources yet (oil, steel, rubber), no loans or Lend-Lease, and the computer never disbands a formation it cannot pay for.
- The rest of free play: seaborne invasions planned by the computer, alliances the computer makes and breaks for itself, peace short of capitulation, and a staff for the air and the sea.
- The rest of the United States: it is an army in North Africa and an industry off the map, and no more. It cannot be played, builds nothing, sends no Lend-Lease, and its neutrality before Pearl Harbor is not modelled. Japan and the Pacific are not in the game.
- The rest of the unit stories: the computer never spends an honour, keeps a rivalry or raises a lost corps again, air fleets and fleets have no veterans-and-drafts rule of their own (pilot training lives in the air pool), admirals and air commanders have no ranks, and there are no decorations for single generals. In history's plans no Allied air fleet flies a bombing offensive yet, so the tour-of-duty honour is earned by the computer only for Germany; a player who orders bombing earns it for his own crews.
- The rest of the commander system: skills and traits for admirals and air commanders (today they gain levels from their battles, and an admiral can be lost with his flagship), command limits, rivalries and politics between generals, and staff college. Generals lead single corps today, because the prototype has no armies above the corps yet.
- The rest of the campaign screen: buildings a province can be given beyond fortifications, armies above the corps with one general over several corps, and a peace conference when a long campaign is won.
- Graphics. This is deliberately a staff map. The Total War and Company of Heroes level of presentation arrives with the Unreal Engine 5 build.
- Borders are approximate: regions are grown from seed points, not traced from 1939 maps.

## Run it

```
npm install
npm test            # flight model, war economy, the budget and the ledger, the arsenal, research, diplomacy, eastern front, desert and Balkans rules, generals, the war at sea, the war in the air, the plans' orders of 1942 at home and overseas, the United States, Torch and Tunisia, Kursk and the Dnieper, Sicily and Italy, Italy in 1944, the West in 1944, the eastern front of 1944, free play, the grand campaign's menu and later start years, the flags, campaign goals, the order to stand or fall back, unit stories, saved games and the fourteen exit tests
npm run build       # writes dist/play.html, a single file you can open in a browser
npm run history     # prints a full computer-vs-computer war; add "sweep 20" to compare seeds
npm run geo         # regenerates the maps from data/campaign/regions.mjs
```

## How it is laid out

| Path | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| `data/` | Moddable data: nations, regions, seas, starting armies, fleets and air fleets, the historical plans in Europe and in the side theatres, the staffs' tempers and choices for free play, the eastern front and desert rules, generals, unit stories (honours, citations, letters), research, aircraft |
| `src/core/` | Shared deterministic core: seeded random numbers, maths, the Battle Ticket contract |
| `src/campaign/game.ts` | Campaign rules: war, movement, supply, weather, battles, mobilisation, capitulation |
| `src/campaign/economy.ts` | The treasury: income, province yields, upkeep, debt and the ledger; replacements, new corps and fortifications |
| `src/campaign/arsenal.ts` | The arsenal of the grand campaign: the list of offers both interfaces draw from (`offers`, `orderOffer`), aircraft, ships and works on order, and where each can go |
| `src/campaign/research.ts` | Research and doctrine: design teams, history's clock, what a lead is worth |
| `src/campaign/diplomacy.ts` | The alignment track, courting, and history's events |
| `src/campaign/navy.ts` | The war at sea: fleets, sea control, fleet actions, convoys, troopships and landings |
| `src/campaign/air.ts` | The war in the air: air fleets, range, radar, missions, the sky, bombing and strikes |
| `src/campaign/goals.ts` | Campaign goals: the short and the long campaign of each playable nation, and how they are judged |
| `src/campaign/commanders.ts` | Generals: experience, skills, traits, ranks, wounds and capture, promotion |
| `src/campaign/stories.ts` | Unit stories: service records and diaries, honours and what the player does with them, veterans and drafts, the memorial wall, home ties, rivalries, letters |
| `src/campaign/save.ts` | Saved games |
| `src/campaign/ai.ts` | The computer's generals |
| `src/campaign/staff.ts` | Free play: the staff that decides for a computer-run nation when no plan is followed |
| `src/campaign/turn.ts` | The monthly turn and the player's orders |
| `src/campaign/ui/` | The campaign screen |
| `src/air/` | Flight model and air battle groundwork |
| `tests/` | Rule tests: flight model, war economy, the budget (province yields, upkeep, debt, the ledger), the arsenal (what needs industry, artillery, wings, ships and shipyards, guns and airfields, saves), research, the war at sea, the war in the air, saved games, diplomacy, the eastern front, the desert and the colonies, the Balkans and airborne troops, the eve of war, the orders a plan can give at home and overseas, the United States, Torch and Tunisia, Kursk and the Dnieper, Sicily and Italy, Italy in 1944 (`anzio.test.ts`), the West in 1944 (`west.test.ts`), unit stories (`stories.test.ts`), free play, campaign goals, the orders to stand, dig in or fall back, Poland on the eve of war, generals |
| `tests/exit/` | The framework's exit tests |
| `tools/` | Map generation and the history runner |

Everything in the simulation is deterministic: the same seed and the same orders always replay the same war. That is what will make saved games, replays and multiplayer possible, and it is why the rules can be ported to Unreal without changing their results.

## Roadmap (from the framework)

1. **Phase 1, now.** Campaign rules and auto-resolve in a browser map (done, and now deepening with production, saved games, diplomacy, the eastern front of 1941, generals, the war at sea, the war in the air, research, the Mediterranean and North Africa, Greece and the Balkans, the eastern front of 1942 and Stalingrad, the desert of 1942 and El Alamein, the United States, Torch and Tunisia, Kursk and the road to the Dnieper, Sicily and Italy in 1943, Italy in 1944, and free play from September 1939), and a gray-box air battle with 300 aircraft (paused).
2. **Phase 2.** The Battle of Britain as a vertical slice in Unreal Engine 5: one campaign turn, one playable air battle, the full Battle Ticket loop, on licensed or commissioned art. Its campaign half (radar, airfields, the aircraft and pilot pipeline, Dowding's groups under Park and Leigh-Mallory, the Channel battles and Sea Lion as a what-if) is already playable on the browser map, and its exit test passes there.
3. **Later phases.** Land battles, the war at sea, the full campaign systems, the other theatres.
