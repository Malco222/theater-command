# Theater Command 1939-1945 (working title)

A grand strategy game of the Second World War in Europe and the Mediterranean: a turn-based campaign map on top, real-time battles underneath, joined by one contract called the Battle Ticket. The design framework (v9) is the source of truth for what the game is.

This repository holds **Phase 1** of the framework's roadmap: the campaign rules and auto-resolve on a simple browser map, built so the same rules and data can be carried into the Unreal Engine 5 build in Phase 2.

## What works today

**The campaign prototype** (open `dist/play.html` after a build)

- Europe, North Africa and the Middle East from Ireland to the Volga and from Norway to the Sahara in 375 regions and 22 seas, generated from real coastlines (Natural Earth, public domain). Moscow, Stalingrad, the Caucasus oilfields, the Crimea and Turkey are on the map, and so are French North Africa, Libya, Egypt and the Suez Canal, the Levant, Iraq, Gibraltar, Malta, Crete, Cyprus and Rhodes.
- 31 nations, 30 with their September 1939 armies as corps on the map, reinforcements by month, and replacement pools fed by industry. The thirty-first is the United States, which is off the map in history's scenarios and comes in later. The grand campaign is played on a wider chart with North America on it (479 regions and 44 seas; the United States, Canada, Mexico and Cuba at home: Version 74, below).
- Monthly turns starting September 1939. Each corps gets four moves a month; fast troops in a fresh offensive get more.
- Armies as pieces. Each nation's force in a region stands on the map as one piece: its flag of the time on a standard (the Kingdom of Italy's Savoy arms, the 48-star flag, the hammer and sickle; Germany flies the black, white and red tricolour with an iron cross, as most games of the period do), a small plate on the flag's corner when its strongest corps is armoured, motorized, cavalry, mountain or fortress troops, its senior general's name over it, a bar under it for the strength it has left. Click one of yours to pick it up: a tray along the bottom of the map shows the general and the army's corps as cards, and a click on a card leaves that corps behind. Right-click a region to march there or to plan an attack on it; the road the army would take is drawn as the pointer passes over, and the piece is seen to march. On a touch screen a tap or a press-and-hold does the same. Fleets and air fleets fly the same flag over their markers, and it stands beside each nation's name in the panels.
- The face-off. Before an attack the two armies are drawn up against each other: each side's general, men, divisions and corps as cards, the ground between them, and a balance bar with a mark for the strength it takes to carry the position. Click your corps to send them in or hold them back, then auto-resolve or withdraw. The result comes back on the same screen with each corps's losses and what became of it. Fighting the battle yourself is the 3D build's job; the button is there and switched off.
- The province. A selected region shows what it holds as four slots (industry, works, harbour, airfields). On your own home ground the works slot is a button that builds the next level of fortification, and a row of cards raises new corps on the spot; whatever is forming there is listed under them. The rules are the war economy's own: this is the Build tab's orders given where the troops will appear.
- The other nations' month, watched. When the player ends the month the map travels to each nation that takes ground or fights a battle, says in a line what it did, and moves on; a Skip button plays the rest at once, and the opening screen has a switch to turn the watching off. The dispatches that follow put declarations of war, fallen capitals and capitulations on cards of their own, above the rest of the news.
- Campaign goals. Every nation a person can play has a short campaign and a long one (`data/campaign/goals.json`): cities to hold, nations to beat, and a date. Germany's short campaign is what history's Germany did, Warsaw, Oslo and Paris by the end of 1940. Poland's goals are counted in weeks, four to a month: the short one is to be in the war still when October opens (history's Warsaw surrendered on 28 September), the long one is to be fighting in the sixth week, longer than history's five, and it is won even if Poland goes down later. The goals are shown on the nation-pick screen for the September 1939 starts, ticked off on the opening screen as the war goes on, and announced in the dispatches when they are won or lost. They judge the war and never change it: no computer nation knows about them. A campaign that opens later leaves out goals whose date has gone by.
- Alerts and keys. A column of alerts on the map says what wants attention this month (armies at the front that have not moved, armies cut off from supply, a treasury in debt, money unspent, generals with skill points, an idle design team), and a click on one goes there. Enter ends the month, Escape backs out of whatever is open, and Space skips the watching.
- Stand, dig in or fall back. Every army of yours carries a standing order for the day the enemy comes, set with three buttons in the army tray. An army told to stand fights where it is. An army told to dig in does so: once it has sat a whole month on the same ground without marching or fighting it defends 25 percent stronger, the Battle Ticket says so, and it loses the fieldworks the moment it leaves them. An army told to fall back gives ground when the attack arrives with odds of 1.2 to 1 or better: it slips away whole to the ground behind it, loses some cohesion and the region, and no battle is fought. It does this once a month (caught again, it stands), fortress troops always stand, and nobody gives up their own capital this way. The ground given up is filed with the battles as "Fell back". The orders are the player's alone: no computer nation uses them, so history's wars are unchanged.
- Friend, enemy or neutral. Click any territory and a banner under its name says where the nation holding it stands with you: Yours, Ally (green), Enemy (red) or Neutral (grey), with a line that says why ("Germany is at war with you. Also at war with France.", "Italy is a member of the Axis, but is not at war with anyone.", "Hungary is neutral, and leaning toward the Axis."). An enemy is a nation you are at war with; an ally is a nation on your side of the war; everybody else is neutral, whatever bloc they sit in. On occupied ground the banner also says whose ground it is and where they stand. The same word rides on the label under the cursor, and it changes the month the war does. With nobody at the controls the banner names the bloc instead (Axis, Allies or Neutral). The rule is `standing` in `src/campaign/diplomacy.ts`.
- Supply traced from each nation's own capital, industry and depots. Armies that are cut off fight at half strength and waste away.
- Battles settled at once from a Battle Ticket (forces, terrain, forts, supply, air, commanders, tempo, direction of attack) with the framework's victory levels. Fortifications only count against the direction they face, so the Maginot Line can be outflanked.
- Nations ask for terms when they have lost too much of their capital, territory and army. France's fall creates Vichy; eastern Poland goes to the Soviet Union under the 1939 pact; sea-supplied armies can be lifted off from a port.
- Computer opponents that follow history's plan as data (`data/campaign/plan-historical.json`). The plan sets intentions only; every region still has to be won.
- A war economy. Factories pay into the treasury every month (half speed at peace, half value on occupied ground). Losses are made good first; what is left buys new infantry, mountain, motorized and armoured corps and fortifications, which take months to arrive. Computer-run nations start ordering once their scripted mobilisation is over. All costs are data (`data/campaign/production.json`). Money is shown at a hundred to the production point, so an infantry corps costs 4,800.
- The budget, in the grand campaign. Every province pays something to whoever holds it: a factory 100, a city 30, farmland 18, forest 12, hills 10, marsh and mountain 6, desert 3, a harbour 12 more, an oilfield 150, the capital 200. Conquered ground and colonies pay half. Every corps, ship and aircraft draws upkeep from the treasury each month (an infantry division 4, an armoured division 10, a battleship 8, a hundred bombers 10), halved for a nation at peace. Income less upkeep is what is left for replacements and new orders. A treasury that runs dry goes into debt: no replacements go out, nothing can be ordered, unpaid corps rest badly and lose men, and creditors stop lending at three months of income. Computer-run nations order nothing they could not keep in the field. `ledger(g, nat)` in `src/campaign/economy.ts` returns the whole of a nation's books for either interface to show; the numbers are in `data/campaign/budget.json`. The historical scenarios keep the plain arithmetic of factories, so that history's timetable holds.
- The ledger. The treasury is always on the top bar with what the month will add or take; a click opens the ledger: what is in hand, income, upkeep and what is left, one bar showing how much of the income the army, navy and air force each take, money in by source, money out by arm, and every province held with what it pays, richest first. A selected province shows what it is worth to its holder and what it would pay in the player's hands.
- The arsenal, in the grand campaign. The treasury buys more than corps. The Build tab has four shelves. **Army**: infantry, mountain troops, artillery, motorized troops and tanks. **Air force**: fighters and bombers by the wing of a hundred. **Navy**: destroyer and submarine flotillas, cruisers, carriers, battleships, and troopships that add to the sea lift. **Works**: factories, shipyards, anti-aircraft guns, airfields and fortifications, built in the province picked on the map. Every offer shows its price, how long it takes, what it costs to keep, where it would go and, when it cannot be ordered, why not. A selected province shows the same things as slots and cards: what can be raised, stationed, laid down or built right there.
- Heavy equipment needs industry. Infantry and mountain troops can be raised in any home province; motorized troops and artillery need a factory in the province, tanks two. Aircraft need an aircraft industry (a home province with two factories for fighters, three for bombers) and airfields to be stationed on: a province where one of the nation's air fleets is based, or one where airfields have been built, in which case the first wing founds a new air fleet there. Ships are laid down in a home port whose shipyard is big enough: level 1 for destroyers, submarines and troopships, level 2 for cruisers, level 3 for carriers and battleships, which take about two years. History's shipyards are on the map (`yards` in `data/campaign/arsenal.json`) and a yard can be enlarged.
- Factories are built in cities (a province that is urban, the capital, already has industry or is a place of some size), at most three new ones in a city and two in a town, each dearer than the last. A factory adds a production point to the income every month and counts toward what the province can raise.
- Artillery is a corps of massed guns. In the attack each artillery division takes 15% off the worth of the defender's fortifications and fieldworks, up to 60%; in the defence each adds 5% to the other defenders, up to 20%. It is slow and weak if caught alone. The Battle Ticket has a line for it.
- Every arm has its ground, in the grand campaign. The classic rules make a mountain or a city hard to take and cut a tank attack to 60% in mountain, city and marsh. On top of that, from `data/campaign/ground.json`: tanks attack 20% weaker in forest and 10% weaker in hills, and defend 25% weaker in mountain and marsh and 15% weaker in forest; motorized troops and artillery are weaker in mountain and marsh; cavalry is weak in a city; mountain troops attack 30% stronger and defend 25% stronger in mountains, and a little stronger in hills. Off the roads (entering ground that was not friendly before the month began) tanks pay one more move in mountain, forest and marsh, and lorries and guns one more in mountain and marsh. Ten regions are too steep for tanks altogether: the high Alps (Tyrol, Alto Adige, Graubunden, Savoy), Narvik and Nordland, Sukhumi under the main ridge of the Caucasus, Montenegro, Epirus and Van. An armoured corps cannot march, go by rail, be shipped or attack into them, cannot retreat there and cannot be raised there; the map marks them with a pair of peaks and shades them when tanks are picked up beside them. Click any province to read how its ground treats each arm, and the Battle Ticket has a Ground line.
- Combined arms, in the grand campaign. Infantry attacking with tanks beside it hits up to 15% harder and the tanks up to 10% harder. One armoured division supports about three of infantry, and one division of infantry covers one of tanks, so a few tanks among many infantry help only part of it. Tanks sent into a city without infantry lose a further 25%, into a forest 15%. In defence, infantry with tanks in reserve behind it is 8% stronger, and tanks holding a city or a forest alone are weaker. Infantry here means foot infantry, mountain troops, motorized troops and cavalry. The Battle Ticket has a Combined arms line that says which of these applies.
- Rivers and bridges, in the grand campaign. Thirty-nine great rivers are drawn on the map from `data/campaign/rivers.mjs`, and wherever one runs between two neighbouring regions there is a crossing with its bridges (111 of them, in `src/generated/campaign-rivers.json`). A corps that attacks across standing bridges fights at 95%. While the bridges are down nothing on wheels or tracks crosses in either direction: tanks, motorized troops and artillery cannot march, attack or go by rail over that river, and infantry crosses in boats, a move slower and at 70% in the attack (85% with engineers in the assault). Bridges go down three ways. Troops who lose a bank blow the bridges behind them if their side still holds the far bank: a 65% chance with troops there, 95% with engineers, 25% with nobody, and 35 points less when fast troops (tanks, motorized, cavalry) took the bank, who are on the bridges before the charges are fired. The player blows them by order from a province on the bank (the province panel lists each river and has the button). A computer nation on the defensive blows the bridges in front of a force half as strong again as its own, and never the ones its own offensive means to cross. Each bridge rolls dice of its own, so nothing else in the war shifts. Nothing rebuilds itself. The numbers are in `data/campaign/engineering.json`.
- Engineers, in the grand campaign. An engineer corps (one division, 18 points, three months, from the Army shelf; Germany, the Soviet Union, France, Britain and Italy start with two each) is the only thing that repairs or builds in the field. Given a job in the province where it stands, it is done when the owner's next month begins: rebuild every bridge that is down on that province's rivers, relay a railway that has been cut, put one bombed factory back to work, fortify the province by a level (as far as level 2; heavier works are ordered from the treasury), lay minefields and booby traps (two belts at most, each taking 12% off any attack on the province and costing the enemy a move to enter it, lifted when the province is lost), or demolish fortifications a level. A corps can be put to work on the day it arrives, and loses the job if it leaves. Engineers work among the other troops: a province takes two engineer corps over its five. Sent in with an attack instead, engineers breach fortifications (each division takes a quarter off what the works are worth, as far as half, on top of what the guns do), clear lanes through mines, and get infantry over a river whose bridges are down. The computer sends its engineers by rail or road to the repairs it wants most (bridges an offensive is waiting at, then cut lines at the end of its working net, then its own bombed factories) and sets them to work on arrival; with none to see to, they dig in and mine where they stand next to the enemy.
- Railways, in the grand campaign. The net is on the map: 476 lines laid by `tools/build-rails.mjs` from `data/campaign/rails.mjs` (a line to every province outside the desert by the easiest country, main lines between neighbouring cities, each country's own net joined up, and the trunk lines history built), in `src/generated/campaign-rails.json`. Where a river runs between two provinces the line crosses by the bridge. Troops go by rail only along it, and each province's line is either working or cut: no train runs into, out of or through a province whose line is cut, and none crosses a river whose bridges are down. Only an engineer corps can be carried up to the break itself. Lines are cut three ways. Troops driven out of a province tear up the track as they go: a 40% chance while their side still has troops next door, 15% with nobody near, and 20 points less when fast troops took the province. The player tears up a line by order from the province panel, with troops on the spot. Bombers that get through cut the line of the province they bomb (one bomber in nine hundred is a point of chance, 85% at most, less what anti-aircraft guns stop), and a province with a railway and no industry can be bombed for that alone. Each province rolls dice of its own. Nothing relays itself: an engineer corps takes a month. An army lives off its railhead, the nearest province joined by working, friendly track to one of its depots or supplied ports. Two provinces of road beyond it cost nothing; at three or four the army attacks at 85% and defends at 92%, further out at 70% and 85%, and the Battle Ticket has a Railhead line. The desert keeps its own line, counted from the ports, and an invader inside the Soviet Union keeps his, counted from the frontier, but there a line the defenders tore up stays unconverted until engineers relay it. The computer's armies ride as far as the line runs and march on from there. The numbers are in `data/campaign/railways.json`.
- Difficulty, in the grand campaign. Five levels named for ranks, chosen on the new-campaign screen and shown as a badge beside the date: Cadet, Captain, Colonel, General and Field Marshal. Colonel is the fair fight, with nothing added, and is what a campaign starts on. A level never touches the battle arithmetic: an attack is worth the same at every level. It changes what each side can afford and how the rival staffs think. On Cadet and Captain the player earns 30% or 15% more and his replacements arrive that much faster, while rivals refill their ranks more slowly and wait for better odds before they attack. On General and Field Marshal rivals earn 20% or 40% more, refill 25% or 50% faster and open their offensives sooner. Every computer nation flies air raids at every level (see the air war below); a rival sends half as many on Cadet and a quarter fewer on Captain, and on General and Field Marshal half or two thirds of its bomber groups go out each month in place of a third, it bears heavier losses, and a target on the player's ground counts for more with it. On Field Marshal a rival with shipping also keeps two corps by a home quay and lands them on a harbour the player has left thinly held, then feeds the harbour it has won, and a rival opens an offensive against the player at 80% of its usual ratio while an ally of its own already has one under way. A rival is every computer-run nation that is not on the player's side; the player's allies, and neutrals leaning his way, always play the fair game. The level is saved with the campaign and can be changed in any month from the badge. The numbers are in `data/campaign/difficulty.json`. In trial wars to the end of 1944 with the computer playing Germany as if it were the player, Berlin fell in 5 wars of 48 on Cadet, 8 on Captain, 13 on Colonel, 19 on General and 29 on Field Marshal; with the Soviet Union in its place, Moscow fell in none of 24 on Cadet, 4 on Captain, 6 on Colonel, 10 on General and 12 on Field Marshal. The outcomes of single wars swing widely, so a level is judged over dozens of them.
- Declaring war by marching, friendships, and the price of a surprise. Pick up an army beside a neutral frontier and the neutral ground in its reach shows in amber. Send the army across and the game asks first: "Declare war on Denmark?", with the country's friends, the bloc it will join when attacked, and what the neutral world will make of it, country by country. Say no and the army stays where it was; say yes and war is declared and the army attacks or marches in. The same prompt opens from the declare-war list. In the grand campaign a blow against a country at peace with the attacker and all his allies is a surprise: for that month the attacker's assaults on that country's ground are 15% stronger (a Surprise line on the Battle Ticket), for every nation, the computer's included. When the player strikes a country that belongs to neither bloc, every neutral still at peace moves 8 points of opinion away from his bloc (and 2 points of the leaning it drifts back to, so part of it is lasting); the victim's friends move 25 and 10. A neutral leaning his way may step back and stop trading with him, and a doubtful one may begin to lean toward his enemies and in time join them. Countries have friends, shown on the Powers screen and in the prompt: the Nordic countries, the Baltic states, the Low Countries, the Balkan Entente and the Iberian neighbours stand together, and there are single friendships such as Britain and Portugal or Poland and Romania. The numbers and the friendships are in `data/campaign/relations.json`. Both interfaces use the same calls in `src/campaign/turn.ts`: `invasions(g, corps)` for the ground that means war, `previewDeclare(g, on)` for the prompt, and `orderDeclare(g, on)` to go through with it. In 72 wars with nobody playing, the surprise bonus left the balance within its usual scatter: Berlin fell in 16 (18 without it), Moscow in 19 (15), and Paris fell in the same month on average.
- Small countries and the League, in the grand campaign. Sixteen smaller nations can be played from September 1939, on a second row of the new-campaign screen: Belgium, the Netherlands, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland, Spain, Portugal, Switzerland, Hungary, Romania, Yugoslavia, Bulgaria, Greece, Turkey and Ireland. A small country in the player's hands chooses its own path from the **Your path** card on the top bar: join the Axis, join the Allies, stand alone, or found or join the League. Joining an alliance cannot be undone. Nobody chooses for it: opinion does not drift it along the track, nobody courts it, no treaty signs its land away or annexes it (Moscow's demand for Bessarabia is refused, and whoever wants the land must take it), and if it is attacked it stays its own master until the player takes a side. The League of Free Nations is a third alliance, of smaller nations, that belongs to neither bloc and shows on the map in a colour of its own. Its members are allies of one another, war declared on one member by an outsider is war with them all, none of them can be courted or annexed by treaty, and a member that goes to war of its own accord goes alone. The great powers neither found it nor join it. The player founds it with any willing partner and, as a member, invites others for 3 political capital each; a country says yes if it is a friend of a member or has a nation at war on its border, and no if it is at war, leans toward a bloc or sees no danger, and the card gives each country's answer and reason before anything is spent. For computer-run nations it is rare: a circle of friends still outside both blocs founds it with a 25% chance in the month after one of their friends is attacked, or with one chance in a thousand a month unprovoked, and afterwards a friend of a member joins with 6% a month and a country with the war on its border with 1.5%, up to eight members. A computer-run staff takes a decision to attack a member the others would stand by half as often. The declaration prompt names the members who would come in. The numbers are in `data/campaign/league.json`, and both interfaces use the calls in `src/campaign/turn.ts`: `paths(g)`, `leaguePartners(g)`, `orderPath(g, id, partner)`, `leagueInvites(g)`, `whyNotLeagueInvite(g, nat)` and `orderLeagueInvite(g, nat)`, with `leagueMembers(g)` and `LEAGUE.color` in `src/campaign/league.ts`; `bloc()` answers `'league'` for a member. In 72 wars with nobody playing, a League formed in 14, most often Sweden and Finland after the attack on Denmark and Norway, and the wars ended much as before: Berlin fell in 14 (16 without the League), Moscow in 18 (19).
- The technology tree, in the grand campaign. Research there is a tree of 46 technologies in five branches (land, air, naval, industry, doctrine) and four steps, read from `data/campaign/tech.json` by every version of the game. Each research team works on one technology at a time; the great powers begin with two teams and the rest with one, and the Research institutes and the Central laboratories each add another. A technology takes 5, 8, 11 or 14 months by its step, opens when the ones before it are done, and takes half as long again for every year it is begun before its own. What it gives lasts for the rest of the war. Fifteen technologies raise one of the eight branches of weapons by a level, worth what a design ahead of history is worth there (Medium tanks, Heavy tanks and Late-war armour each make armoured and motorised corps fight 8% harder; Machine tools, the War economy and Mass production each raise earnings 5%), and two make oil at home (Synthetic fuel and rubber, then Hydrogenation plants). Others work the way a national ability does and are named on a battle's ticket: Mobile warfare and Close air support for the fast troops, Heavy and Rocket artillery for the guns, Defence in depth behind fortifications, Landing craft and Amphibious assault in a landing, cheaper tanks, aircraft and ships, faster replacements, fewer cargoes lost. Four hold back an order until they are researched: armoured corps wait on the Tank arm, motorised corps on Motorised infantry, carriers on Carrier aviation, and the new airborne corps (two divisions that go in by air, two at a time) on Airborne forces; the Build tab lists a held order with a lock, and a click on it opens the tree at the technology it waits on. Winter equipment ends the eastern winter's penalty; the Soviet Union and Finland begin with it. Every nation begins with what it had in 1939 (Germany its tanks, staff colleges and airborne troops; Britain radar, sonar and carriers), a campaign begun in a later year gives each the months of research in between, and the United States goes on researching at home before it enters the war. A computer-run nation keeps every team at work, choosing by step and by its own order of preference among the branches (Germany the army, Britain the navy, the Soviet Union industry), and takes nothing years ahead of its time while something of its own time is open. The Research tab lists what the teams are doing and what is open; the tree itself is one screen with a lane for each branch, lines from each technology to what it opens, and the chosen technology spelled out underneath. The historical scenarios keep research on history's clock.
- World Domination, the last act of the grand campaign. The war between the alliances is decided when a great power (Germany, Italy, Britain, France, the Soviet Union) has fallen and no two great powers still standing are at war with each other. In the month that happens a card puts the choice to the player: **take the victory**, which leaves the alliances and the campaign as they are, or **World Domination**. Choosing World Domination cannot be undone. Every alliance is dissolved, the League with them; every nation still standing, yesterday's allies among them, is at war with every other; troops on a former ally's soil go home under safe conduct; a nation whose homeland lies beyond the map (today the United States) takes its forces home and is out of it. From then on nobody is courted, no treaty is signed, no armistice is made (a France conquered now is simply conquered) and nobody makes peace again: the last nation standing has won. The computer's staffs fight it one neighbour at a time: each country along a frontier is a front of its own, weighed against what the others could do meanwhile, and a staff opens an offensive at 85% of the odds it would want in an ordinary war. The Powers tab becomes the standings: every nation still standing ranked by its share of the world's worth, with its provinces, its army (as well as the fog of war lets it be known) and how much fight it has left, and below them who fell to whom. The top bar counts the nations left, and when the player is the last one a card says so. A player who takes the victory can still choose World Domination in any later month, from the Powers tab. Computer nations never choose it, so a war with nobody at the table ends as it always did. Every number and every line of news is in `data/campaign/domination.json`; the rules are in `src/campaign/domination.ts`.
- Spies and the fog of war, in the grand campaign. The player no longer sees the whole map. A foreign army is in view only on the player's own side's ground, beside its troops and one province beyond, and what it is made of is known only once it is identified (see "Fog on armies" below); a province further off it is a flag and a count of corps; deeper than that the map is veiled and nothing is known. A new tab, **Spies**, is the secret service. Agents are recruited for 600 each (Germany runs four at once, Britain and the Soviet Union five, France and Italy three, a small country two), train for a month at home, and take a month on the road to any province on the map, where each stands as a small seal that opens its card when clicked. An agent at work is a pair of eyes: it reports every army in its province and the provinces around it, lists what is on order and which air fleets are based where it stands, and the player's armies attack that province 8% harder, with a line on the battle's ticket. In a capital an agent can read the government's books: treasury, income and upkeep, the true size of its army, what it has on order, what its laboratories are working on and what is in its depots; without that, a foreign army is known only to the nearest ten divisions. Against a country the player is at war with, an agent can wreck a factory (it comes off 45% of the time), cut the railway (55%) or fire an oilfield (45%; the wells give half for three months). The attempt is made once, as the month turns; then the agent lies low for two months and the province is on alert for three. Every month abroad an agent may be caught: 3% watching, 7% reading the books, 14% to 18% on the night of an attempt, half as much again in a capital, a quarter more in a country at war, 60% more in a province on alert, and every chance is shown before the order is given. A neutral that catches a spy protests, and its opinion moves six points away from the side that sent it. Agents learn: three points of experience make an Operative and eight a Master spy, each rank a fifth harder to catch and surer of hand. The other side has services too: from the fourth month of the campaign every great power at war with the player has a 5% chance each month (none on the easiest level, half on the second, more on the hard ones) of wrecking a factory or cutting a line on the player's home ground, and an agent left on guard at home stops 70% of the plots in or beside its province. The fog can be lifted with one button under Spies by a player who wants the open map. The computer's staffs see the whole map as before and keep no agents on it, so a war with nobody at the table is fought exactly as it was, and the historical scenarios have neither fog nor agents. Every number is in `data/campaign/intel.json`; the rules are in `src/campaign/intel.ts`.
- Oil and supplies, in the grand campaign. Money builds an army, oil moves it, and supplies keep it fighting. The six great powers (Germany, Britain, France, Italy, the Soviet Union, the United States) each keep a stock of oil and a stock of supplies beside the treasury, shown as two gauges on the top bar; every number is in `data/campaign/resources.json`. Each month what a nation makes and brings in goes into its depots and what it needs comes out. Oil comes from oilfields (Baku 40 a month, Ploiesti 14, Grozny 12, Iraq 12, Maikop 8; a conqueror gets 60%), from small wells and plants at home, and from the tree's fuel plants, which make oil for every working factory; it is burnt by tanks and lorried troops (half a point a month for an armoured division), aircraft, and ships, far more at sea than in harbour. Supplies come from farmland, factories, colonies and occupied ground, and go to the people at home and the army in the field; Britain's own ground covers half of what it needs. The rest comes from abroad. Six shipping lanes cross the sea and are drawn on the map: the Atlantic lane brings Britain 95% of its oil and 62% of its supplies, and loses cargoes to raiders exactly as the convoy war counts them (raiders against escorts and aircraft in the North Atlantic, the Western Approaches and the Irish Sea). Italy's lane is shut by whoever holds Gibraltar, Germany's by a blockade for as long as it is at war with Britain or America, and the Arctic convoys run only once the Soviet Union fights beside the western Allies. Overland deals bring Romanian oil to Germany and Italy for as long as Ploiesti is in no enemy's hands, Soviet oil and grain to Germany until the two are at war, and Swedish ore by whichever way is open. When a stock runs dry the forces get only what arrived that month: with half the oil, tanks and lorried troops attack 25% weaker and defend 12% weaker (and lose a move below half), aircraft are worth 25% less, fleets are 20% less fit for sea; with half the supplies every corps fights 15% weaker, 40% fewer replacements go out and the treasury earns 25% less. A battle's ticket says when either side is short. Months of empty depots become privation at home, which wears away the will to fight and counts toward surrender beside a lost capital, lost ground and a broken army: a country that imports half its food and is cut off for a year and a half asks for terms. That is how a blockade chokes a nation. Click either gauge for the depots card: both sets of books line by line, every lane and deal with the share getting through, and how the other powers stand as far as it shows from outside (an enemy's stock is its own secret). The Navy tab lists the lanes to guard and the lanes to raid, an oilfield's panel says what it yields and to whom, and alerts warn when a stock has six months left. Computer-run staffs put fuel chemistry first when their oil is running down and stop raising tanks they could not run. Smaller countries buy what they need abroad and are never short. The historical scenarios do not use any of it.
- National abilities and special formations, in the grand campaign. Every country a player can lead, and the United States, fights, builds and earns in its own way, from one row per nation in `data/campaign/traits.json` that every version of the game reads. In battle an ability multiplies the attack or defence of the nation's corps where its conditions hold (the kind of troops, the terrain, home ground, fortifications, the winter months, a landing, the years): Germany's tanks and motorised troops attack 15% harder in open country, the Finns defend their own country 25% harder from December to March and attack 15% harder in forest, Switzerland defends its mountains 30% harder. In the books it adds income (Swiss banks, Swedish ore and the Turkish straits while at peace; the Norwegian and Greek merchant fleets, the Congo and the East Indies in peace and war), doubles what Romania's oilfields earn, keeps a quarter of the Soviet factories an enemy has overrun at work beyond the Urals, and raises American earnings by 15% once the United States is at war; each has its own line in the ledger. Others make orders cheaper (Soviet infantry, French and Belgian forts, British warships, German submarines, American bombers), make good losses faster (the Soviet Union, by a quarter), add shipping (Norway, Greece, Denmark, Portugal), strengthen the Royal Navy by a tenth and cut its convoy losses by a fifth, or mean nobody catches Poland by surprise. Occupied Yugoslavia fights on: every enemy corps in its mountains, hills and forests loses 4% of its strength a month. Thirteen countries can raise a special formation from the **Build** tab, marked with a star, a few at a time: German heavy tank corps and Soviet Guards from 1942, Commonwealth corps, the French colonial corps, Italy's Alpine corps, American marines, Polish cavalry brigade groups, Finnish ski corps, Belgian Ardennes rifles, the Spanish Legion, Hungary's rapid corps and Greek Evzones. Italy, Poland and Greece start with one. The computer raises them too. The new-campaign screen lists what the chosen country does best, the **Abilities** card (top bar, **Build** or **Powers**) shows any country's list with what each ability does worked out from its own numbers, a special formation carries its star on its card, and a battle's ticket names the abilities that count in it. Three abilities the rules already carried are listed with the rest: Germany's lightning war, Britain's radar chain and the Red Army in winter. The historical scenarios know none of it.
- Factories in ruins, in the grand campaign. A raid that stops more than 8% of a province's output has twice that share as its chance of wrecking one factory outright, as far as half the province's factories. A wrecked factory pays nothing into the treasury until engineers rebuild it, one a month, and the province panel counts the ruins.
- Anti-aircraft guns, levels 1 to 3, ring a province's industry: each level stops 15% of any bombing of that province and brings down more of the bombers, and the Battle Ticket of a raid says so. Airfields, levels 1 to 3, let a new air fleet be founded in a province for fighter patrols, and each level lets 500 more aircraft fly from it. Both cost a little every month to man, and the ledger lists them. A computer-run nation puts guns on its richest factory province once it has been bombed. These are campaign rules: the month's air campaigns are still settled from a Battle Ticket.
- The war at sea. Eighteen fleets start the war in their 1939 bases, each a count of battleships, carriers, cruisers, destroyers and submarines under a named admiral. A fleet patrols a sea to hold it, raids shipping, or lies in port to refit. Fleets that meet fight a fleet action settled from its own Battle Ticket, and aircraft flying from the coast count toward holding a sea, so the narrow waters belong to whoever owns the airfields. The naval rules are data (`data/campaign/navy.json`, `data/campaign/seas.json`).
- Fleets as pieces, in the grand campaign. The navy is handled like the army. Click one of your fleets on the map and it is picked up: its ships are laid out in the tray as cards by class (battleships, carriers, cruisers, destroyers, submarines) with what each is for, and the seas it can reach are ringed. It takes one of five orders (`data/campaign/fleets.json`). **Patrol** holds a sea and brings enemy fleets to battle. **Raid** goes after merchant ships. **Escort** binds the fleet to one shipping lane: it sails with the convoys and guards the lane in full along its whole length, each destroyer counting 1, a carrier 3, a cruiser a half and a battleship nothing, where a fleet that merely patrols one of the lane's seas gives a quarter of that. The lane picker shows the share of cargoes being sunk now and with this fleet as escort. **Hunt** sends destroyers and carrier aircraft after submarines, which nothing else can sink: each month a nation's hunters in a sea sink a share of the enemy submarines there that grows with the destroyers hunting, the carriers, the aircraft on the coasts and radar, and never passes a quarter. **Port** brings it home to refit. Heavy ships need a screen of one and a half destroyers each; a fleet short of them where submarines are at work may lose a battleship, carrier or cruiser to a torpedo, and the tray shows the screen as a bar. Battleships and cruisers at sea off an enemy coast can shell it once a month, and a coast is shelled once a month: the troops there lose organisation and some of their men (less in cover, dug in or under concrete), or the factories of a city stop part of their output for a month, and a fortified coast answers. The plan shows what the guns should do before they open, and every bombardment files a Battle Ticket. Ships can be split off as a fleet of their own (an escort fleet, a hunting group, a bombardment force) and fleets in the same place joined. Computer admirals escort the lanes on their beat and shell coasts their armies stand beside, and a fleet lying in harbour puts to sea for the month to do it where its side would be half as strong again as the enemy in that sea; a fleet at sea sees the coasts of its sea through the fog. Notes on the map say when a coast of yours has been shelled and when a lane of yours is losing cargoes with no escort. Over 72 computer wars the outcomes are level with before, and in two-year wars 64 to 73 submarines are sunk, close to history's count. The history campaigns are untouched.
- Carriers and the air war at sea, in the grand campaign. A carrier is a floating airfield. Every fleet with carriers brings a wing of fighters and a wing of strike aircraft (`data/campaign/carriers.json`: 12 and 24 to a British carrier, 30 and 48 to an American one), laid out in the fleet's tray as cards beside its ships and listed under **Air** as "Your carriers". They go where the fleet goes. At sea a wing reaches every coast of the sea it is in, the ground inland as far as its range, and the seas next door; in harbour it flies from its port like any airfield. The raid planner has two new kinds of raid. **Strike a fleet** sends bombers against one enemy fleet at sea, and **Raid a harbour** against the warships lying in one port: the plan shows the chance of success, the bombers likely to be lost and the ships likely to go down before anything flies, and every strike files a Battle Ticket that names the ships under attack and what was sunk. Hits are counted from the bombers that get through; carriers and battleships are aimed at first; a battleship takes more sinking than a destroyer; and no strike sinks more than about a third of a fleet at sea or half of one at anchor. Carrier fighters over a fleet break up a strike (even a few spoil a good deal of an unescorted one), the ships' own guns bring bombers down, and an escort of fighters cuts what the cover spoils. Carrier fighters also keep the enemy's coastal bombers off their fleet month by month. Bombers on land can strike ships too, but few crews are trained for it: a British or German land bomber counts about a tenth of a carrier aircraft against ships. Ships in harbour cannot manoeuvre and are hit half as hard again, and a harbour raided in the month war is declared is caught unready: its fighters and guns count a quarter and the hits are trebled. Wings are made up to strength only while the fleet lies in harbour; a new carrier joins with its aircraft aboard; a carrier sunk takes its aircraft with it, but for the two in five that land on another deck or ashore; and a carrier with empty decks is worth far less in a fleet action, with a convoy or hunting submarines.
- Finding ships at sea, in the grand campaign. A fleet has to be found before it can be struck, and the player sees only the enemy ships his side has found. A nation's search of a sea is the ships it has there (a carrier counts most, by its aircraft), its carriers in the seas next door, its aircraft on that sea's coasts, and any search flown there that month: any air group or carrier wing can fly one search a month, beside its raid. A fleet is identified where the search is strong and only reported where it is thin (a flag and a question mark on the map: ships are there, not what they are); open ocean needs three or four times the search of narrow waters, and submarines alone three times as much again. A fleet that fights, shells a coast or strikes with its aircraft is identified by its enemy that month and reported the month after. Ships in harbour are always reported, and identified where the port is in view; a country at peace hides nothing in its harbours. A strike on a fleet that is only reported finds it with three fifths of its bombers. The Navy tab lists enemy fleets as found, a sea opened on the map says how well it is searched, and the computer plays by the same rule: its strike wings go only for fleets its side has found, its land bombers only for fleets it has identified, and a fleet struck from the air keeps out of that sea for four months unless its homeland is being invaded across it. In computer wars of forty months the staffs fly 13 to 26 strikes on ships and sink 19 to 40 of them, and over 72 computer wars the land war is level with before (Berlin falls in 20 against 18, Moscow in 17 against 12, Germany is deep in Russia in 15 against 21, Paris falls in 72 against 71). The history campaigns are untouched.
- Towns you build up, in the grand campaign. The chief place of every province has building plots, more the bigger it is: one in a village, two in a town, three in a city, four in the capital. Click a province and they are under **The town**, beside its works. Each plot takes one line of building, raised in three levels, one building going up in a town at a time: **Commerce** (market hall, merchant quarter, exchange) pays into the treasury every month, more in a rich place, and makes trade worth more; **Farming** (cooperative farms, granaries, food works) sends supplies to the depots, or money in a country that keeps no stocks; **Learning** (technical school, polytechnic, research institute) quickens every research team; **Training** (drill ground, barracks, officer school) gives a corps raised in the province more experience and speeds replacements; **Medicine** (aid post, military hospital, convalescent home) gets a corps resting in the province or beside it more of its men and its rest back; **Administration** (town hall, prefecture, governor's office) brings tax and political capital at home and makes a conquered province pay more of what it is worth. Commerce needs a town, learning a city, farming farmland, forest or hills; on conquered ground only administration and medicine can be set up. A plot once built on keeps its line, so each place has to be given a purpose. **The town plan** is a card that shows the plots and every line with its levels, price, time and effect, or the reason it cannot be built; the Build tab has a **Towns** shelf with what all the buildings bring in and the staff's pick of the best place for each kind. Buildings change hands with the town. Computer-run nations set aside a tenth of their income and build to a mix weighted toward commerce, with one gang of builders and one more for every twelve home provinces. Lines, prices and effects are in `data/campaign/buildings.json`.
- Trade agreements, in the grand campaign. Two countries at peace can sign one, and it pays both every month: the more the richer the two are. Each agreement needs a trading house: a great power has three, any other country two, and every merchant quarter in a harbour town or the capital opens one more, up to eight. Goods take whichever open way leaves most of the worth: across the border where home ground touches (nothing lost), by sea from harbour to harbour (worth less the more seas are crossed, and enemy ships and aircraft sink a share on the way), by a border between colonies, or overland across up to three countries at peace with both, each taking a toll. War between the two, the fall of either, or feeling in a neutral running strongly against the other's alliance ends an agreement, and one with no way open pays nothing while that lasts. Agreements history had standing in 1939 are signed when the campaign opens (Swedish ore to Germany, Britain with France, Portugal and Norway), and every country fills the rest of its houses with the best partners it can find. **The trade office** (from the Build tab's Towns shelf, the ledger or the Powers tab) lists the agreements in force with their route, what each pays and what is being sunk, the houses in use, and every country that would sign with what it would pay; signing costs political capital. Routes are drawn on the map in the wealth view, amber where raiders are at work, and a note says when a trading house stands idle or an agreement is cut. Computer-run countries sign with whoever pays them most and never with the player unasked. Over 72 computer wars the outcomes are level with before. The numbers are in `data/campaign/trade.json`. The history campaigns keep the flat trade they always had.
- The war in the air. Twenty-two air fleets, each a count of fighters and bombers at the airfields of one region under a named commander: the Luftflotten, Fighter Command's groups, Bomber Command, the French air zones and the Soviet district air forces. Left alone a fleet supports the armies and defends the sky within reach of its fighters. Given a mission it fights a month-long air campaign, settled from its own Battle Ticket: win the sky over a region, bomb its industry by day or by night, or strike at shipping in a sea. The air rules are data (`data/campaign/air.json`).
- Range, radar and airfields. Fighters are at full effect to 60% of their range and useless beyond it, so bombers sent further go alone and suffer. Radar gets every defending fighter up in time; a defender without it meets raids with 70% of his fighters. The airfields of one region serve about 1,500 aircraft, and fleets packed in beyond that cannot all fly. Moving base costs readiness, and so does every month of operations.
- The air war as its own game, in the grand campaign. Aircraft are based on **airfields**, which are places on the map: a pad of grass with its runways, bigger for a bigger field (a landing ground serves 500 aircraft, an airfield 1,000, an air base 1,500). The war begins with an air base at every capital and wherever history based an air fleet, an airfield in every city and a landing ground in every town, 126 in all. Engineers lay out a new one in a month and can enlarge it once; the treasury builds larger. No aircraft can be based where there is no airfield, and a field with more aircraft than it serves flies only a share of them. Every air fleet is split into **air groups** of one kind, fighters or bombers, of at most 250 aircraft, numbered the way each air force numbered its own (Jagdgeschwader 3, No. 2 Bomber Group, Groupe de Chasse 1). Clicking an airfield opens it: the groups based there, what each is doing, and its orders. Between raids a fighter group **patrols** its sky (it meets any raid within its reach), **sticks with a named bomber group** as its escort, or rests; each choice says what it would mean before it is given. A **raid** is planned from its target: pick the kind (day bombing of factories and railways, night bombing, a raid on troops, a fight for the sky, a shipping strike), pick the target on the map or from the list, and the planner shows the enemy fighters that will rise to meet it, the chance of success, and the bombers and fighters likely to be lost. Then tick who goes. Bombers from several airfields **meet up** and lose a smaller share than a group sent alone; fighters in range go as escort and leave their patrol for the month. The raid is flown the moment it is ordered, from the same Battle Ticket as before, and every group that went is away until next month. A **raid on troops** cuts the organisation of the corps in a province (up to 35%, less in cover and dug in) and costs them some men and guns, so the army attacking them afterwards meets a weaker defence. A **reconnaissance** sends 12 aircraft over a hidden province: every army there and beside it is seen whole until the month ends. The planner's chance of success was checked against 489 raids flown by four nations over six months: raids it gave 10 to 25% won 15% of the time, those it gave over 90% all won, and the losses it forecast came within 3% of those flown. **What a raid costs** is counted nearer history's: a day raid that goes without escort into a strong patrol loses 15 to 25% of its bombers, an escorted one 3 to 8%, and a night raid 2 to 5% (it was half the bombers before); over a raid the fighters of the two sides fight half as hard as in a fight for the sky. **The computer's nations fly raids of their own** at every level of difficulty, flown at once like the player's: each month about a third of a nation's bomber groups go out, each with the fighters on its own airfield as escort by day, against the factories and railway junctions of its enemies, by night where the day would cost too much, or against the armies on a front where the fighting is; its fighters fight for the sky only over an offensive of its own. A staff flies a raid only where the forecast says it should pay and the bombers' losses stay under a tenth, and it hits no place twice in a month. A quiet front stays quiet in the air. On Cadet and Captain a rival sends fewer raids; from General it sends more, bears heavier losses and goes for the player first. Raids on the player are in the month's dispatches, and a note on the map names the provinces hit. Checked over 72 wars with nobody at the table: about five raids a month across the map, and the war's endings moved little (Berlin falls 18, Germany deep in Russia 23, Moscow falls 8, stalemate 23, against 20, 14, 12 and 26 before).
- The fall of nations and their return. Every country that leaves the war is **announced** on a card of its own, with its flag, who it fell to, when, how many home provinces went with it, and whether it can come back: beaten in the field, annexed by treaty, or out of the war by the terms of a peace. Several in one month share a card, a row each. That part runs in every campaign. In the grand campaign a country beaten in the field is **liberated** when a member of an alliance at war with its conqueror holds its capital: take Paris from Germany as Britain and France returns to the war beside the Allies, takes back every province of its own that its new allies hold, puts a small new army in the field at its capital (one to three corps by the size of the country), and is run by its own government from then on; whatever its allies win back of its ground afterwards is handed over to it at once, and so is ground the armistice gave to Vichy. A conqueror's own fall frees what it held: when Germany is beaten, the countries whose capitals pass to its enemies return the same day, and Germany's card lists them. The Axis frees countries too (Germany retaking Rome brings Italy back beside the Axis), and the League frees small ones. A freed country stands for as long as its capital does and can fall a second time if the capital is lost. A player whose own country has fallen watches the war go on and is **given the country back** the day it is freed. On the map the capital of every fallen country the player could free is ringed in gold; its province panel says what taking it would do, and the Powers screen lists the fallen with the same line. Not freed: a country annexed outright or divided by treaty, a country whose capital is taken by a nation outside every alliance (that is a conquest), a great power by the League, anyone in the month it fell, and anyone once the last act has dissolved the alliances. Numbers and words in `data/campaign/liberation.json`.
- **Armies opened up: divisions, morale and rest.** Click an army and the tray along the foot of the map says what it is made of, arm by arm ("4 armoured, 2 motorised and 3 infantry divisions"), with one card for each corps and inside it a chit for every division: its number and name (7th Panzer Division, 263rd Rifle Division), its strength along the foot, and a stripe for each grade of experience from Green to Elite, gold-edged once it is battle-hardened. The divisions of a corps always add up to exactly the corps, and each keeps its own character through the war, so the same division is always the most worn or the most seasoned of its corps. Every corps shows three meters: **strength**, **rest** (the cohesion it always had, under the name the player reads) and **morale**, with an arrow for which way morale is going and the reasons behind it in words. In the grand campaign morale goes up with victories (more for a decisive one, and for taking an enemy capital), on home soil, with friendly troops in the province or next door, under a general the men trust, and after a quiet month; it goes down with defeats and routs, heavy losses, being cut off, going unpaid, a winter without winter kit, standing alone in front of the enemy, and the fall of the nation's own capital. Morale is worth up to 15% either way in a fight and steadies or unsteadies the ranks, and the battle ticket has a line for it. A corps that spends a whole month in supply **without marching or fighting rests**: its drafts arrive half as fast again, it gets back more of its rest, and its spirits recover; the tray marks such a corps *Resting*. The historical scenarios keep no morale and fight exactly as before; they show the divisions and the rest meter all the same. Numbers and words in `data/campaign/morale.json` and `data/campaign/divisions.json`.
- **Cities and towns: where the valuable places are.** Every province has a chief place drawn on the map at a spot of its own: a cottage for a village, a few roofs round a spire for a town, a skyline for a city, and a domed hall under a star for a capital. The more gold on it, the richer the ground. A province named for a stretch of country shows its chief town under its name (Essen in the Ruhr, Breslau in Lower Silesia, Milan in Lombardy), and beside it what it pays its own country a month, with signs for factories, harbour and oilfield. Cities and capitals show their figures at the usual zoom and everything shows closer in. The coin button above the zoom buttons turns on the **wealth view**: every province tinted by what it pays, poor ground dimmed and rich ground gold, so the places worth fighting for stand out across the whole map. Click any province and its panel opens with **the prize**: what the place gives you if it is yours, or what taking it would bring, in one sentence ("Taking Warsaw is worth 115 a month to you and costs Poland 430: 2 factories and 6 victory points of Poland's 16. It is Poland's capital.") and then item by item: money, industry, oil, harbour, airfield, railway junction, victory points, a capital, a fallen country it would free, a fortress in the way. Ground won back for an ally says that it goes back to the ally. The same sentence is on the card that plans an attack, the battle report says what the ground just won now pays, hovering over a province gives it in a line, and the orders panel lists **the richest prizes within reach**, the most valuable first. None of this changes a rule: it says what was already true of the ground, in every campaign. Names and size rules are in `data/campaign/towns.json`.
- **Fog on armies: you know an enemy only as well as you have looked.** Seeing a foreign army's flag is not knowing what stands under it. A foreign corps is identified in three ways: by fighting it (both sides of a battle learn each other, and allies share what they learn), by an agent at work with it in view (its own province and the ring around it), or by a reconnaissance flight over its province (and the ring around that). In the month it is identified the report is exact: kind, divisions, strength, rest and morale, down to the division cards. After that the report goes stale. For four months the corps keeps its name, its kind and its divisions, but its strength is an estimate inside a band that widens 15% a month, and its rest and morale read "Not known"; then it is unidentified again. An army that stands in view of the player's lines without a report shows its flag, a count of corps and the staff's estimate of its strength (`~5` under the flag, "about 4 to 6 divisions" in the panel); a province further off it is a flag and a question mark. The card that plans an attack is honest about it: against a defence that is not fully known the odds are a band ("Odds between 1.7 and 2.6 to 1"), hatched on the balance bar, the verdict is the staff's opinion ("The staff thinks the odds favour you", "The staff cannot call it"), and the lines for the defenders' numbers, spirit and armour say what is not known. Every band holds the truth, and it is the same all month, so it cannot be narrowed by asking twice. A button on the province panel and on the attack card sends the nearest air group that can photograph the province, and the card redraws with the exact odds; reconnaissance is now offered over ground in view wherever an army there is not identified. Lifting the fog under Spies shows everything, the computer's staffs see everything as they always did (so a war with nobody at the table is fought exactly as before), and the historical scenarios know none of it. The numbers are in `data/campaign/intel.json` under `reports`; the rules are in `src/campaign/intel.ts` (`identify`, `reconnoitre`, `corpsKnown`, the bands) and the masked cards in `src/campaign/armies.ts`.
- **Joint armies: allies in one group.** Allies already shared a province and defended it together; now they attack together too, as one army under the player's hand. Where an ally's field corps stands in a province with the player's own, the tray and the province panel offer **Join my army** (one corps, or every allied corps there at once). A corps that joins keeps its own flag, pay, drafts, supply line and general, but takes the player's orders in the player's month: it is picked up with the army, marches and goes by rail with it, attacks beside his corps, and the ground they take together is his. The army's standard on the map flies the ally's colours in its corner, the tray shows whose each corps is, and the card that plans an attack is headed for both nations ("German and Slovak army"). An ally lends only what it can spare: 40% of its field corps (at least one, never more than eight), and nothing while it is at war with none of the player's enemies; a corps in a joint army stays out of a fight with a country its own is not at war with. It does not sail, drop from the air or dig works on the player's orders. Its own staff leaves it alone while it is away. It goes back when the player sends it (**Send back**), when it is left for a month with none of his troops beside it, or when the two countries are allies no more. No computer nation forms a joint army and the historical scenarios have none, so those wars are fought exactly as before. The numbers are in `data/campaign/joint.json`; the rules are in `src/campaign/joint.ts`.
- **Sieges: the town is the key to the province.** In the grand campaign a province no longer changes hands the moment its army is beaten. Troops stand either in the field or inside the town as its garrison (a village or a town holds one corps, a city two, a capital three, fortress works one more), and every town of the country it belongs to has a guard of its own: depot troops, police and militia. Win the field and the army closes round the town. The province is then under siege: it pays nothing, raises nothing and runs no trains, and it becomes yours only when the town is stormed, starved out or opens its gates. Troops inside fight 10% stronger in a village, 40% in a town, 70% in a city and twice as strong in a capital, and more behind fortress works. A village or a town held by its guard alone opens its gates to an army with odds of 4 to 1; a city or a capital never does, and has to be stormed or starved. A town is assaulted once a month by every corps round it, whether or not they have marching time left. An assault in the month the army came up is a hasty one and goes in at 80% of its weight against a village, 70% against a town, 50% against a city and 40% against a capital. Tanks in the streets count for 75%, luck runs against the attacker (an even chance stands at 1.33 to 1 where it is 1.2 to 1 in the open, and the attack card shows it), and an assault costs 60% more men than a fight in the open. An army that closes round a held town marches no further that month, so nobody drives through a defended country in one bound. A closed ring eats the town's stores. The ring is closed when the army round the town has three quarters of the strength inside, counting its side's troops in the provinces next door at half their weight, or when every road out already runs into ground the enemy holds: a month for a village or a town, two for a city, three for a capital, and a month more for each level of works. A port the defenders' ships can reach is never starved while one sea off its harbour is open to them: a fleet strong enough to close those seas starts its stores running down, and the siege panel names them. A garrison may sally out once a month, and a relieving army attacks the besiegers in the open field with half the garrison's strength added to its own. Air raids (a new target, the town) and a fleet's guns break down up to 70% of the works, shake the troops inside and thin the guard; left alone, a town mends 20% a month. A full town takes no more troops: new armies form up on the nearest ground their side still holds. A capital closely ringed counts nothing toward its country's fall in the month the ring closes, half lost after three months, and more each month until after a year it counts as lost outright, so a capital stands a siege and no siege of one lasts for ever. A country does not give in while its capital stands free and less than 60% of its home is lost, and a beaten country gives in to the enemy at its throat (`victorOf`), never to whoever's month it happens to be. A town left with nobody in it falls to the army round it when the month closes. On the map a besieged province is tinted in the besieger's colour, with a turning ring on the town and a shield that counts its garrison; the province panel, the tray, the assault and sortie cards, the shelling card and the battle report say the rest. The computer garrisons its cities, invests a city in strength, calls up corps from three marches away, storms at good odds (and at shorter ones the longer it has sat there), sallies when it can win, marches to the relief of a capital at shorter odds than of any other town, and bombs the towns its armies stand round. The numbers are in `data/campaign/siege.json` and the rules in `src/campaign/siege.ts`; the historical scenarios are untouched.
- **The war keeps moving.** In the grand campaign a war nobody is fighting does not stay that way. The staffs' monthly chances are real throws, one for each month, so the great choices (the attack in the west, the attack on the Soviet Union, Italy's wars) come in different months from one war to the next and sometimes not at all; a blow that is ready can still be put off a month by weather or nerves, and a staff launches one great operation a month. A quiet front makes bolder generals: after nine months without a land battle a staff opens its offensive on four fifths of the advantage it would usually wait for, after fifteen on two thirds, and a staff standing on the defensive takes a weak point in the line where it sees one. A nation with shipping that has been at war for a year with no land front keeps three corps by a quay and crosses the sea to open one. The player is told once a year while his own war is silent, and what that silence will bring. A rich country at war keeps more corps forming at once (one line for every six factories it holds), and the standing call-up shrinks with the factories lost. Enemy troops standing on a neutral's ground cannot be attacked until war is declared on the neutral (`whyNotAttack`), and the forecast on the attack card counts only the corps the ground will let in. News that repeats month after month (the same raid on the same town, the same escorts in the same sea) is told in full the first time and folded into one line after that (`routineNews`, `foldNews`). Over 72 computer wars to the end of 1944 the east opens in every one (55 before), and stalemates there fell from 25 to 8: Berlin falls in 40, and Germany is in Moscow or deep in Russia in 24. Over twelve wars run to 1947 no siege lasts longer than two years. The numbers are in `data/campaign/open.json` (`impatience`, `descent`, and each choice's `chance` and `hold`) and `data/campaign/siege.json`; the historical scenarios are untouched.
- **Prizes outside the town, and scorched earth.** In the grand campaign a province's oilfield, its factory district, its airfield, its harbour and its great rail junction are places of their own (`prizes.ts`, numbers in `data/campaign/prizes.json`): 5 oilfields, 37 factory districts (half the factories of any province with two or more), 126 airfields, 89 harbours and 32 junctions where five lines or more meet. What lies outside the walls goes with the country round the town. An army that wins the field holds those places at once and has the use of them after a month, while the town still holds: the district's factories pay it, the wells give it their oil, its aircraft fly from the airfield, and its trains run through the junction though the station in the town is shut. The harbour of a city, a capital or a fortress lies inside the defences and stays with the town; the quays of a smaller port are lost with the field, and its garrison is no longer fed by sea. A place can be wrecked by the army that holds it: by order, a corps' work for the month (the wells burn for 8 months, a district stands idle for 6, a harbour is blocked for 5, an airfield ploughed up for 3, a junction is cut until engineers relay it), or as that army is driven off, if its nation has ordered scorched earth there. The player sets a policy (never, on conquered ground, everywhere) and can give any province an order of its own. Each place then burns about 85 times in 100 when troops are still at hand, 35 with nobody near, and 25 fewer when tanks, lorried or airborne troops took the ground; a hasty job, out for 2 to 5 months. Scorched earth takes the wells, the district, the airfield and the harbour, and tears up the railway as the province falls: a hasty cut the new holder's railwaymen make good in two months, beside the rule the railway always had when ground is lost. A besieger driven off by a relieving army burns what he held in the same way. Time mends a wrecked place and engineers standing there mend it a month faster each month. Computer nations have policies of their own: the Soviet Union burns everywhere, Germany and Britain burn what they conquered, the rest burn nothing. The province panel lists the places with who holds each and how it stands, with the buttons to wreck one and the scorched earth order; the map shows a flame where something is wrecked and a pennant where an army holds places round a town it has not taken; the ledger has a line for what those places pay. Both interfaces read `prizesOf`, `prizesOnMap` and `myPrizeIncome` and give the orders `orderWreck`, `orderBurn` and `orderScorchPolicy` (`turn.ts`). The historical scenarios have none of this.
- **Government, and directives from the war cabinet.** In the grand campaign every nation has a form of government (`government.ts`, numbers in `data/campaign/government.json`): a parliamentary democracy, a one-party state or an authoritarian government, each with its own temper. The player's has a war cabinet of four offices: Chief of the General Staff, Minister of Armaments, Foreign Minister and Minister of Finance. Each stands in a caretaker's hands until he appoints one of two candidates, and every candidate brings a gain and a price: the organiser sends replacements out 15% faster and costs political capital, the planner lifts income 5% and makes new corps dearer, the taxman lifts income 6% and wears down the cabinet's trust. An appointment costs 6 of the political capital that also courts neutrals and signs trade agreements, and the office then stays four months. Decrees put the country on a war footing one step at a time, for political capital: a war economy and then total mobilisation (income up 6% and 12%), general conscription and then the last levy (replacements 20% and 45% faster), the hardest steps only once the country has been at war a year or has lost home ground, and each with a price in trust or income. The cabinet's trust in the player runs from 0 to 100 and moves each month with the news: provinces won, home provinces lost, battles won and lost, an enemy capital taken, a treasury in debt, his own capital under siege, and what his ministers and decrees add or take; a democracy feels all of it a quarter more, a one-party state a quarter less. With no news it settles back toward 55. A cabinet that doubts him (under 35) withholds half his monthly political capital, one that believes in him (75 or over) adds to it, and one with no trust left for three months running falls: caretakers take every office, the newest decrees are withdrawn and his political capital is spent. Every few months the cabinet, or an ally with its back to the wall, sets a directive chosen from the state of the war: take a town in reach, hold a threatened city, bring the army up to strength, or strike the enemy pressing an ally. It has a last month and a reward: political capital, money for the treasury, or from an ally a corps. A directive is the player's to carry out, ignore or set aside, but each one ignored in a row costs more trust than the last and lowers the level trust settles at until one is carried out. The **Cabinet** button on the top bar opens the Government card: the trust meter and what moved it, the directive with its clock, the four offices, the two decree ladders, what it all comes to and the cabinet's minutes. Computer nations keep a caretaker cabinet, so their wars are fought exactly as before, and the historical scenarios have none of it.
- **Prepared defences: a line that is dug in can hold.** In the grand campaign an army left where it stands for a whole month, neither marching nor fighting, digs in, whoever commands it: the computer's armies as well as the player's, and with no order given (`digIn` in `game.ts`, numbers in `data/campaign/defence.json`). Troops inside a town do not, since its walls are their works, nor troops under orders to give ground, and the fieldworks are lost when the army moves. The works take three months to finish, and a corps counts by the share of them it has had. Against a line that is dug in and in supply the day's luck runs against the attacker: in the open it runs from 0.8 to 1.2 and it takes 1.2 to 1 on the day to carry the ground, so one and a half to one cannot fail; against a finished line it runs from 0.5 to 1.2, so that the same attack carries the ground a little over half the time, two to one about six times in seven, and nothing is safe under 2.4 to 1. The attackers lose half as many men again and the defenders a third fewer, and an attack thrown back from such a line has no moves left for the month. Three things take a line apart. Massed guns take their share off it as they do off fieldworks. The first two months of a new offensive by an army trained to it (Germany's) catch it off balance, and take 85% off. Tanks break into a line with no armour of its own, and take half. The attack card shows the chance before the player commits, with the white mark at the odds that are an even chance; the Battle Ticket has a line, Prepared defence, that says how ready the line is and what it costs; an earth bank is drawn under the flag of an army that is dug in; and the order for an army that is attacked is now one button, Stand and dig in, beside Fall back. Both interfaces read `Assessment.dice` and `Assessment.prepared`, `BattleReport.prepared`, `Corps.dug` and `Corps.dugFor`, and the helpers `chanceOn` and `evenOdds`. Measured over 72 computer wars to the end of 1944, against Version 57: Berlin had fallen in 25 (38 before), the German army stood deep in Russia in 7 (16), and in 40 (10) neither; Paris falls two to three months later. A German player on the middle level, following history's plan, held Berlin to the end of 1944 in 7 wars of 12 (6 before), with most of the falls between January and May 1945, where history put it, and threw back about 1 attack in 9 on his prepared lines (none before). None of those twelve was still standing in December 1945 (2 before): the Red Army still arrives at two and a half to three to one, and numbers decide. On the playtester's nine wars, which include two riskier plans, Berlin was held to the end of 1944 in 3 (4 before). The historical scenarios keep the order to dig and their old luck.
- **What the playtester found, put right (Version 58).** A capital no longer counts half lost the month the ring closes round it: it counts nothing that month and grows to half over three, so Rome stands a siege before Italy asks for terms. A staff driven off a town by a relieving army leaves it alone for a season unless it has much the stronger force. Scorched earth now slows an advance: troops with the order tear up the railway as a province falls, on the same chance as they burn its places, beside the rule the railway always had; such a hasty cut needs no engineers, since the new holder's railwaymen make it good in two months, but inside the Soviet Union the line is converted only three months after that, so burned ground keeps an invader's supply line long for longer. The month's burning is told in one line for each army driven off, with the airfields counted and not named, and airfields alone are no longer news. The places outside a town are announced in the month they start to pay, with what they are worth, not when they are seized. A wreck button asks once more in red before anything is blown up, a greyed one says why in words, and the country's scorched earth policy is folded behind one button, which takes 75 to 110 pixels off every province panel.
- **Manpower: no nation has men without end (Version 59).** In the grand campaign every nation has a reserve of men fit to bear arms (`manpower.ts`, numbers in `data/campaign/manpower.json`), counted in divisions' worth: Germany opens September 1939 with 1.6 million, the Soviet Union with 5.6 million, a small country with a few tens of thousands by the army it keeps. Replacements for worn corps and every new corps are drawn from it, the Soviet standing call-up included, and each month a class comes of age and joins it: 36,000 men a month for Germany, 105,000 for the Soviet Union, less as home ground is lost (counted by the worth of the provinces, never under a tenth). A reserve that is spent makes good only what the month's class allows and raises no new corps, so a war of attrition is lost by the side that runs out of men first. The rule is the same for the player and the computer: a computer staff raises new corps, and the Soviet call-up forms new armies, only with men its armies in the field are not waiting for. The player sees it as a fourth gauge on the top bar, **Men**, beside the treasury, oil and supplies; the ledger has the count (the reserve as a bar against what it opened with, the month's class, what has been called up); every corps on the Build list says what it takes in men and refuses with the figures when the reserve has too few; the news and an alert warn at a half, a quarter and when it is spent. The call-up decrees enlarge the class by 15% and 40%. A campaign that opens after 1939 finds an eighth of each warring nation's reserve gone for every year of war behind it. Measured: the Red Army, which grew to 740 divisions by the end of 1944 and past 1,100 by 1947 with nothing to stop it, now stands at 300 to 480; a German player who follows history's plan at the middle level holds Berlin at the end of 1944 in 13 wars of 24 (the target is about half); and all 36 computer wars run to January 1947 are decided, 33 with Berlin fallen and 3 with Moscow, where 7 of 36 were still frozen in Version 58.
- **The computer's guns (Version 59).** A computer staff whose line has met dug-in troops now orders massed guns: once three enemy corps beside its ground are dug in it raises a corps of artillery for every six of the rest, up to ten (`arsenal.json`, `ai.guns`), and its offensives put the guns first into any attack on works or a fortress. - **The war cabinet after its playtest, and a switch to play without it (Version 59).** The playtester played Version 57 through 197 wars and found the cabinet asked for the wrong things and cost nothing to ignore; all thirteen findings are answered. The cabinet now reckons the odds before it names a town: the player's troops in and beside the province, and half of those two provinces off, over the defenders with their fortress and walls counted (`directiveOdds`). It asks only for towns at 0.6 to 1 or better, marks one under 1.5 to 1 as strongly held and gives it two more months and half as much again, never asks for a town an ally already stands round, and does not name the same town again within a year of a failure while another will do. An order to hold is given only where the enemy stands at the gates or next door in strength, counts only if the place was attacked or besieged while it ran, and is otherwise withdrawn with no reward and no blame. A country at peace is asked to build up its army by no more than its treasury can pay for (counted as the map counts it, corps forming included), or to sign a trade agreement, and for nothing at all when neither can be done. An ally hard pressed names a town held by the enemy pressing it, and one that was overrun asks to be liberated. Ignoring the cabinet now has teeth: each directive missed in a row costs 3, 5, 7, 9 and then 11 points of trust and lowers the level trust settles at with no floor, so an idle player's cabinet falls after four or five, and a cabinet that doubts the player withholds 8% of the treasury's income as well as half his political capital. Setting a directive aside costs half of what failing it would and brings the next no sooner. The money levers have a price: a war economy and total mobilisation cost trust every month and take political capital to keep, and lapse a step when there is none; every price on the card is a figure. An appointment costs a small country 3, every government opens with the capital for one, and an appointment or a decree asks before it spends. - **The cabinet talks back (Version 60).** Three things the playtester proposed and the player approved, all in the grand campaign and all behind the cabinet switch. *Hawks and doves*: every minister but a caretaker sits with one wing, and each office offers one of each. Every point the table leans to the hawks makes ground won and battles won count 15% more with the cabinet and home ground lost 25% more; every point toward the doves makes battles lost count 25% more, costs trust for losing more than 2% of the army's men in battle in a month, and lets trust settle 2 higher while no directive stands ignored. Two more ministers of one wing than of the other and that wing has the table: hawks ask for a town whenever there is one to ask for, doves ask to hold, to build up, to trade, to help an ally and to spare a worn army (a new directive: so many men lost in battle and no more), and for a town only when nothing else fits. A shift in the balance makes the news. *Call your shot* (`promiseTargetsOf`, `orderPromise`): the player may promise the cabinet an enemy town within four provinces of his ground or his troops, in 2, 3, 4 or 6 months, once every 6 months. The cabinet reads the odds on the day (his troops within reach over the defenders, walls counted), the depth and the time, and calls the promise safe, fair, bold or daring: a safe one earns a point of trust, a daring one nine, with political capital and up to most of a month's income for a capital; broken, it costs two to ten points; taken back, half that. Dismissed within two months, its trust rises 5 and it carries what his popularity would have cost; stood by, or left unanswered, its trust falls 4 and it watches him for six months: a battle won makes him *Vindicated* for the rest of the war (trust up 6, his corps 4% stronger in attack and defence, never named again), a battle lost first costs 3 more. - **Fronts that move again (Version 61).** The playtester played Version 58 through to the end and filed nine findings; this is the answer to them, all of it in the grand campaign and none of it in the historical scenarios (numbers in `data/campaign/defence.json`). *Works wear down.* Every attack on dug-in troops, won or lost, takes a month off the works of each defender who stays (`wear`; in full for an attack at 0.6 to 1 or better on the Battle Ticket, in proportion below that, and more with massed guns), and troops who were attacked that month dig at half pace (`Corps.pressed`), so steady pressure wears a line away and a quiet front builds it back. - Aircraft and pilots. Factories and flying schools deliver on history's schedule into a reserve that tops the fleets up each month, resting fleets first. A new fighter needs a pilot. A pilot shot down over his own ground usually flies again; one shot down over the enemy's does not.
- The sky. An attacker wins the sky over a region when the fighters he has left at the end of the month outweigh, by 1.65 to 1, what the defender can still put up once his losses are made good. It lasts a month. An army attacking there has the air on its side, and no invasion fleet can cross to a coast while enemy fighters still hold the sky over it.
- Bombing cuts the victim's next month of production. Strikes on shipping sink warships at sea and coastal trade.
- The Battle of Britain. On history's plan the Luftwaffe fights over the Channel in July 1940, goes for the airfields of Kent and Hampshire in August, turns on London in September and goes over to the night Blitz in the autumn.
- Sea transport that can be intercepted. Troopships need sea lift and a route through seas the enemy has not closed. A crossing through contested water costs men, and an army overseas is supplied only while a port and an open sea link it to home.
- Landings from the sea. Corps in a port can be put ashore on an enemy coast up to three seas away. Troops storming a defended beach fight at 60% strength, unless they land in the very month war is declared, when surprise makes up the difference. Enemy fleets in harbour nearby put to sea against an invasion, and a landing cannot be attempted at all under a sky the enemy's fighters still hold. That is what makes Operation Sea Lion wait on the air battle.

- The convoy war. German submarines and raiders sink shipping on the Atlantic routes every month, and every ton lost cuts British production. Escorts, patrolling fleets and air cover cut the losses and sink U-boats. The U-boat arm grows on history's schedule.
- Swedish iron ore reaches Germany across the Baltic in summer and through Narvik when the Gulf of Bothnia freezes. In the winter months it arrives only while Narvik is in hands that are not at war with Germany, and losing it costs German industry.
- The war overseas. North Africa, the Levant and the island bases are colonies, and the rules treat them that way. A nation stands or falls by its home country, so losing Libya does not bring Italy nearer to asking for terms, and troops overseas are not counted in the army that defends the homeland. New corps are raised at home and sent out; history's expeditions (the Australians, the Afrika Korps, the Eighth Army's two corps) arrive in the month they did, at a friendly port, and only if the war they were sent for is being fought. When France falls the Army of Africa stays at its posts under Vichy. Battles overseas roll their own dice, so a fight in the desert never changes the luck of the war in Europe.
- The desert. There is one road, no water and nothing to live on, so an army in North Africa is only as strong as the port behind it. Every corps' supply line is counted from the nearest working port, a step for every region of desert: Tripoli and Alexandria are great harbours, Benghazi and Tobruk small ones that start as if two regions out. The longer the line, the weaker the attack, the thinner the defence, the slower the replacements and the shorter each bound forward into enemy ground. An ally's port feeds the corps who came to fight beside him. A fortified port keeps two months of stores after the sea behind it is closed, which is what lets Tobruk stand a siege. No railway crosses the desert. All of it is data (`data/campaign/desert.json`).
- Open desert. Infantry on foot outside prepared works has no flank to anchor on: against an attack that includes tanks or lorried troops it defends at 65%, and when it is driven off it loses more men than troops who can drive away. The Battle Ticket says so.

A plan can keep its army `strict`: it attacks only its `targets`, steps into no empty ground beyond them and makes no counter-attack, which is what keeps a front of two thousand kilometres to history's calendar. It may still walk into an island of ground the enemy has left altogether, and against a player it is not bound: ground a human enemy holds is stepped into and won back as freely as by any other army. An event can make `peace` between two nations inside the bigger war (each goes home), a `cede` signs over ground the taker already holds, and a `partition` marked `occupied` leaves each piece of the country to whoever has troops standing in it. A formation an event raises forms at the nearest place behind a front too crowded to form on. Dated works on the main front (`data/campaign/east.json`, `works`) dig the Panther line, the Courland harbour and Budapest's Attila line, in history only. Germany's replacements are shared between the fronts, so the army in Italy mends more slowly while Bagration is on, and the Fifth Army's July attack toward the Arno now waits for better odds (`minOdds` 1.6) so that Tuscany and the Marche still fall together in August.
- Landing ships. Troops fighting their way off a beach count for 60 percent of their strength. From June 1943 British and American troops count for 80 percent, for the landing ships, amphibious lorries and naval gunfire they have by then. Who has them, and from when, is data (`data/campaign/navy.json`).
- Armies made from armies. A formation can be put together out of an army already overseas: the invasion force for Sicily takes its divisions out of the British and American armies in Africa, and they shrink by as much. A corps left with no divisions is struck off, and garrisons are never drawn on.
- The land bridge. Between the bend of the Don and the Volga there is one region, Kalach, and it is the only way to Stalingrad from the west. The Don flank above it and the Kalmyk steppe below it are separate regions, so an army on the land bridge is cut off the moment both go.
- Orders a plan can give (`data/campaign/plan-historical.json`). Besides where to attack and at what odds, a month of history's plan can now say: gather so many tank and lorried corps at a place by rail (`concentrate`), keep so many corps massed at a place whatever else happens (`staging`), leave a stretch of the line to an ally and take your own corps out once he is there (`entrust`), pay a price of its own for one prize (`oddsFor`, which is how Stalingrad is assaulted at odds the plan would refuse anywhere else), stand quiet and attack nothing (`quiet`), and give ground up and march out before the road home is cut (`abandon`). The same orders can be given to an army in a side theatre. The side panel explains each one in plain words to a player whose nation has such an order that month, at home or overseas, and the player is free to ignore it.
- Germany's allies on the Don. The Hungarian 2nd Army, the Italian 8th Army and the Romanian 3rd and 4th Armies arrive through the summer of 1942 and take over the Don front from Voronezh to the Kalmyk steppe, so that the German corps can go on to the Volga. They go in beside whoever stands there, however crowded the place, and what is left of the Hungarians and Italians goes home in the spring of 1943. Italy follows Germany into the war in the east by an event, and only into a war Germany has actually begun.
- News of an encirclement. The newsreel reports the month a ring closes around three corps or more, with the men cut off, and reports the end as one piece of news when they lay down their arms.
- Limited replacements. However rich the treasury, an army gets back only about three men in a hundred of its peak strength each month, shared along the front (the depots' limit; in the grand campaign the men themselves also come out of the nation's reserve, see Manpower below).
- Staffs that learn. The Red Army of 1941 can bring only eight corps into one battle and does not renew a failed assault in the same month. In June 1943 an event remakes it: its troops are better, its air force is the equal of Germany's, it can bring fourteen corps into a battle, renews its attacks and moves reserves by rail like any other army. An event of this kind can remake any nation's army, and it is written in the data: another takes the heart out of the Italian army in July 1943, once Tunis is lost, and its divisions fight at three fifths of what they were worth. Defenders who hold their ground lose fewer men than those who are driven off it.
- Computer generals who bring reserves up behind a spearhead, garrison what they have taken, leave a besieged city alone when the plan says to starve it out, counter-attack ground they have just lost, and close any gap in a line they are standing on.
- Generals. The real commanders of 1939 start the war at the head of their corps, and others wait for a command. A general gains experience from every battle, and each level brings a skill point for one of four skills: offense, defense, logistics and leadership. Traits are earned from how he actually fights: three victories that end in surrender make an encirclement master, three positions held against a stronger attacker make an iron defender, three costly failed assaults make a butcher. Generals can be wounded, captured or killed, and unknown officers make their names in battle and rise from the ranks. The player assigns, relieves, promotes and dismisses them and spends their skill points; the computer does the same for its own. Everything a general adds is shown on the Battle Ticket. All of it is data (`data/campaign/commanders.json`).
- Unit stories. Every corps, air fleet and fleet keeps a service record (battles, breakthroughs, stands against the odds, prisoners, missions flown, tonnage sunk) and a diary, and one that has done enough is honoured. There are 22 honours in `data/campaign/stories.json`, each with a ribbon, a citation and the real precedent it is modelled on, from a capital on the colours to a full tour of twenty-five bombing missions, and an honoured unit may be given a name by its army. The player decides what to do with the fame: carry the honour at the front, where it makes the unit fight a few per cent harder (12% at most for one unit), or send its heroes home, on a bond tour for production points and political capital, or to the schools as instructors so that every later draft and new corps is better trained. A corps that goes home is out of the line for two months. Experience has names (green, trained, seasoned, veteran, elite); green drafts thin out what a corps of the player's knows, and a corps can be told to take none and stay small. A corps defends the region it was raised in harder and loses heart when it falls. One that keeps meeting the same enemy corps makes an old enemy of it, fights harder against it, and is rewarded for destroying it. Destroyed corps go on a memorial wall with their records and can be raised again under the old name, with the old colours and part of what they knew. Up to two letters a month from the player's own units come with the dispatches, and generals hold their army's ranks and are promoted in the newsreel. Records, honours and the wall are kept for every nation, but everything that changes a number applies to the player's own units only, and none of it draws on the dice, so a war the computer plays comes out exactly as it did before (checked for twelve wars to January 1945).
  - For the Unreal build, everything a screen needs is in the saved game. Each unit carries `rec` (its counters), `log` (diary lines as `[turn, what, where, n]`), `hon` (ids on the roll of honour) and `nick`. `stories` holds `awards` (the roll), `fallen` (the wall), `rivals` and `schools`. Honour names, ribbon colours, citations, thresholds, edges and every letter are data in `stories.json`, whose `_about` block explains each key. The player's choices are three orders in `turn.ts`: `orderCash`, `orderDrafts`, and `orderBuildCorps` with a formation from the wall.
- Research and doctrine in the historical scenarios (the grand campaign uses the technology tree instead). The five great powers each have eight design teams, one to a branch: tanks, infantry weapons and guns, army doctrine, fighters, bombers, the navy, radar and signals, and war production. Research runs on history's clock: a branch with one team gets every new design in the month history got it, from the 50 mm Panzer III and the Spitfire Mk V to the Panther, the Lancaster and the T-34/85. A second or third team brings a branch's designs forward, a branch with no team stands still, and extra teams can be founded with production points. What counts in battle is how far a branch is ahead of or behind its own history: each design in service early is worth 5 to 8 percent to the corps, fleets, fighters, bombers or factories that use it until history catches up, and each one missed costs the same. Doctrine is each army's own: Blitzkrieg, Deep Battle, Superior Firepower, Methodical Battle, War of Rapid Decision. The computer leaves one team on every branch, so its nations fight exactly as they did. The Research tab shows every branch as a strip of the whole war, with history's dates, the designers' progress and today marked on it. All of it is data (`data/campaign/research.json`).
- The eve of war. Germany moves first every month, and on history's plan Poland is beaten inside the first one. A player who takes Poland therefore places the army on 31 August 1939, before anyone moves: marches and rail moves on Polish ground, nothing across the frontier, and no month passes. The staff's advice is on the screen (`data/campaign/eve.json`), and one button places the army as the staff would: the strongest corps in Warsaw, the rest on the ground south of it, nobody in the east where the Red Army will cross. What happens next is measured over many wars and held by tests. Left strung along the frontier as it was, the army is destroyed and Poland is out in the fourth week, as in history. Placed as the staff advises, it holds Warsaw through September and falls in the fifth week. Placed so, with the Warsaw garrison told to dig in and the field armies told to fall back when they are outmatched, it is still fighting in the sixth week. Nothing holds Warsaw for good against the whole German army: the aim is to do better than history did.
- The grand campaign is the front door. The start screen asks for a country (Germany, the United Kingdom, France, the Soviet Union, Italy, Poland, or nobody, to watch) and a year: September 1939, or the first of January 1940, 1941 or 1942 (`data/campaign/grand.json`). History is played to that day and then put away: from the day you take over the war is free play, in which you and every computer-run nation's staff decide for yourselves. A country is offered only in the years history still has it in the war, so France can begin in 1939 or 1940 and Poland only in 1939. The years after 1942 are not offered: with nobody holding it to history's calendar the Red Army of 1943 ends the war inside a year, so those years are played as scenarios. The pick is remembered for next time.
- Historical scenarios, on the second menu, where the nations you do not play go on following history's plan. Play Germany, France, the United Kingdom or Poland from September 1939, Germany or the United Kingdom from July 1940 with the Battle of Britain about to begin, the United Kingdom or Italy from September 1940 with the Italian army on the Egyptian frontier, Greece or Italy from October 1940 with the Italian army about to cross the Greek frontier, Germany or the Soviet Union from June 1941, the United Kingdom or Italy from May 1942 with Rommel striking at the Gazala line, Germany or the Soviet Union from June 1942 with Case Blue about to open, the United Kingdom or Italy from November 1942 with the Torch convoys at Gibraltar, Germany or the Soviet Union from July 1943 with the panzers gathered on both sides of the Kursk salient, the United Kingdom or Italy from the same month with the invasion fleet gathering at Malta, the United Kingdom from January 1944 with the Fifth Army about to land at Anzio, Germany or the Soviet Union from the same month with the Red Army about to break the siege of Leningrad, the United Kingdom or Germany from May 1944 with the invasion armies on the quays in Hampshire, or Germany or the Soviet Union from June 1944 with Operation Bagration about to open. You can also watch history unfold from any of the twelve dates, from July 1943 with the map on either Kursk or Sicily, and from January 1944 on either Italy or the eastern front.
- Free play ("open history" in the framework). The war begins as it did on 1 September 1939, with Germany, Poland, Britain and France at war, and after that nobody follows history's plan. Every computer-run nation has a staff that weighs each of its fronts every month: its own attack against the enemy's defence near the line, with forts, terrain, season and air counted. It opens an offensive when the odds reach what that nation's temper asks for (Germany attacks at even odds, France wants two to one), keeps it up while it gains ground, calls it off when it stalls, and rests before it tries again. History's great decisions are written as choices a staff may take up when their conditions hold: Norway, the attack in the west through the Low Countries, the Balkans, the attack on the Soviet Union, Italy's entry and its Greek adventure, a Soviet first strike, and the small Axis allies following Germany east. Each takes months of build-up, has its season, and comes up or not by a seeded draw, and every nation's nerve differs a little from one war to the next. So Germany may go west in March 1940 or October 1941, Italy may stay out, the war in the east may start in 1941, in 1945, from the Soviet side, or never. The events of diplomacy still happen when their conditions come true, but none of them brings a country at peace into the war. Play Germany, the United Kingdom, France, Poland, Italy or the Soviet Union, or watch. Nobody hands the player a plan: the orders panel gives your own staff's reading of the odds on each front instead. All of it is data (`data/campaign/open.json`), and the same seed still replays the same war.

Leningrad, Moscow, Stalingrad and Voronezh are never lost.

- Research moves that balance, and that is tested too. A Britain that put second teams on fighters and radar in 1939 keeps its sky even against all five air fleets. A Germany that put three teams on fighters in 1939 has the Bf 109F in the summer of 1940 and the Fw 190 by October, and wins the sky over Kent with the two western air fleets alone.
- "No two wars alike" (free play, twelve wars from September 1939 to the end of 1944 with no plan followed): Poland falls in the autumn of 1939 in every one. Germany attacks in the west in most, in at least four different months, and France falls where it is attacked in strength. Italy picks its own moment. War comes in the east in most wars, begun by either side and never in the first winter. The wars do not end the same way: Moscow and Berlin each stand in some and fall in others.

Where 1941 still differs from history: Odessa falls a month or two early, Rostov is never reached, Soviet losses are lower than the real ones, and Finland, Hungary and Slovakia declare war but only defend. In the desert, the Axis army gets away from Operation Crusader to El Agheila stronger than the real one did, with about 100,000 men. In the Balkans, Albania is one region, so the Greek counter-offensive that took Koritsa is not drawn: the front stays on the frontier. Crete falls in nearly every trial war, where the real battle was a close-run thing, and the troops sent to Greece are extra to the desert army rather than taken from it, so Rommel's first offensive is not helped by their going. Italy and Bulgaria gain nothing in April 1941, and there are no occupation zones: Germany holds all of Yugoslavia and Greece.

Where 1942 differs from history: the army that is lost is cut off on the land bridge west of Stalingrad, not in the city, which the Germans never enter; it is three to five corps and 100,000 to 270,000 men where the real pocket held about 250,000, and it is gone in January, not on 2 February. There is no relief attempt and no airlift. The Soviet offensive at Kharkov in May 1942 costs 60,000 to 70,000 men, not a quarter of a million. Kharkov changes hands in February and March in about a third of the trial wars and is never lost in the rest. Nothing is planned for the north and the centre in 1942, so there is no fighting at Rzhev or Demyansk and no attempt to lift the siege of Leningrad. In about one war in ten the German army of 1941 never reaches Kharkov or the Donets, and its 1942 offensive starts from further back.

Where the desert of 1942 differs from history: the map has one region between El Alamein and Sidi Barrani, so the Axis line of the autumn stands at Mersa Matruh and the October battle carries that name on its Battle Ticket. The Axis army at El Alamein is about 150,000 men where the real one had some 116,000, and it tries the line in July and seldom again, where history saw a second attempt at Alam Halfa at the end of August. There is no army level above the corps, so Montgomery leads the armoured corps he arrives with. Land battles still take their air support from each nation's air rating, so the Battle Tickets of October give the sky to the Axis, which is wrong. The desert generals of 1940 and 1941 other than Rommel, Cruwell, O'Connor and Montgomery are still unnamed.

Where Torch and Tunisia differ from history: Algiers is too strongly held to storm, so the British land further east, at Constantine, and Algiers comes over with the ceasefire, which arrives when December opens, not after three days. The Levant passes to Britain at the same ceasefire, because the Syrian campaign of 1941 is not in the game. The French who came over fight as one corps under the British flag. Rommel stays in Africa to the end and can be taken prisoner there. After Kasserine the Americans mostly hold their front, and Sfax and Tunis fall to the British: there are no separate battles of El Guettar or Bizerte. The stroke at Kasserine is made by two divisions, and the British take the ground back the same month. American neutrality and Lend-Lease before Pearl Harbor are not modelled, American air power counts only in battles the Americans fight themselves, and the United States cannot be played.

Where 1943 in the east differs from history: the two sides lose about the same number of men from July to December, where the Red Army really lost several times what Germany did, because under these rules the side that wins a battle loses less. Citadel is one battle for the whole salient, decided in a month, so there is no Prokhorovka and no separate northern and southern thrust. Orel falls in July, a month early, and to the fronts from inside the salient. In about half the wars the Red Army walks into the Dnieper bend at Kirovograd in the autumn, two or three months early, because Germany has no infantry left to post there. Kiev falls in November in most wars and in December in the rest. The German stroke at Zhitomir only happens in the wars where the Red Army has the town when December opens, about one in five, and the town changes hands twice inside the month. The Kuban bridgehead, the Mius and the Nevel battles are not fought as such. When Italy leaves the war in September, the share of its air force that counted for the Axis in the east goes with it, which is worth a few percent to the Red Army from then on.

Where Sicily and Italy differ from history: Sicily is one region, so the five weeks of fighting for the island are one battle on the beaches, decided in July; the German divisions that really fought on the Etna line and got away over the strait in August stand in Calabria from the start and are never in that battle. The same is true at Salerno: the Fifth Army meets one panzer division there and takes the whole Naples region in September, where the city really fell on 1 October, and the German counter-attack on the beachhead is not fought. The dice decide very little in this campaign: all sixty test wars ran to the same calendar. After Naples nothing more is fought in 1943, where the Allies really spent November and December forcing the Volturno, the Sangro and the Bernhardt Line at heavy cost. The Italian fleet is laid up in port and does not sail to Malta. There is no Italian Social Republic, no Italian army fighting beside the Allies, no rescue of Mussolini, and no fighting between Germans and Italians in Rome, the Balkans or the Dodecanese. Corsica goes to Britain with Sardinia, because the Free French are not a separate army. The Mediterranean Air Command is British in the game: the American air forces that were half of it are not counted separately.

Where Italy in 1944 differs from history: the dice decide very little here either, and all sixty test wars ran to the same calendar within a month. The four battles for Cassino are four dice throws at one region, so the bombing of the abbey, the Rapido crossings and the fighting in the town are not told apart, and the breakthrough in May is credited to the Fifth Army as a whole, where it was Juin's corps in the Aurunci mountains that turned the line. The beachhead at Anzio is one corps in one small region: it holds or it does not, and the long siege of February to May is a quiet front. The breakout from Anzio in May is not a battle of its own, and VI Corps reaches Rome in June by way of Cassino. Rome falls in June as it did, but with the German 10th Army getting away whole, there is no argument to be had about Clark turning for Rome. The pursuit is quicker on the coast and slower on the Adriatic than it was: Tuscany and the Marche both fall in August, where Ancona really fell in July and Florence in August. On the Gothic Line the Eighth Army really broke through to Rimini in September and the Fifth came within nine miles of Bologna in October; here both are plain defeats in front of Emilia, because the line and the plain behind it are one region. The Germans lose about 115,000 men in the year's battles to the Allies' 200,000, where the real figures were nearer equal: under these rules the side that fails an assault pays for it and the side that retreats pays little. The troops that leave for the south of France in August simply leave the map: Dragoon is not fought yet. There is no Italian co-belligerent army, no partisans behind the German lines and no bombing of the Brenner. The British X Corps under the Fifth Army is an American corps in the game, as it has been since Salerno, and leaves the map in April rather than moving to the Eighth Army.

Where the West in 1944 differs from history: the dice decide almost nothing, and all sixty test wars ran to the same calendar. Normandy is two regions, so the landing is one battle on each beach and the seven weeks of fighting in the hedgerows and round Caen are one failed British attack and one successful American one; the German counter-stroke at Mortain is a concentration of armour that is then beaten where it stands, and is never an attack of its own. The Allies come ashore more slowly than they did, with fourteen divisions in June and twenty-six in July. The British start September on the Seine and cannot reach the Dutch rivers until October, so Market Garden is a month late and is a plain assault from Brabant, with no airborne drop: the Airborne Army arrives in Brussels as infantry. Aachen is part of the Rhineland region, which holds, so the city's fall in October is news and not a change on the map. The Scheldt is not fought for: Antwerp is a working port from the day it is taken. The Atlantic fortresses are stormed in September (Brest, Calais) or starve by the winter (Bordeaux), where Lorient, Saint-Nazaire, La Rochelle and Dunkirk really held out until May 1945. All four of Lorraine, Alsace, the Vosges and Belfort are cleared in November, so there is no Colmar pocket and no fighting on the Saar in December. Liberated France is held by the American and British armies: France is not restored as a nation, and de Lattre's French corps fight under the American flag. The December offensive takes the whole Ardennes region in one battle and does not try for the Meuse or Antwerp; Bastogne is news. The German plan makes no local counter-attacks at all, and the Allied air forces fly no missions after June beyond supporting the armies. The Germans lose about 530,000 men in battle and the Allies about 260,000: roughly half of what the campaign really cost each side between June and January, prisoners included.

Where the East in 1944 differs from history: the dice decide almost nothing, and the twenty test wars ran to the same calendar within a month. Each offensive is one or two battles for a region, so Korsun, Kamenets-Podolsky and the Brody pocket are not told apart, and Bagration is three battles in June and one great surrender at Mogilev in July where it was a dozen encirclements; Minsk falls at the end of June, a few days early. The Red Army is too big and wins too cheaply: it puts nine to twelve million men on the map where it had six and a half million at the front, and it loses about half of what the Axis loses in battle, where in fact it lost far more men than it destroyed. The German army in the east is about 1.3 million men in January where it had over two million. The Warsaw rising and the Slovak rising are news and not battles, and Slovakia stays an Axis country to the end of the story. Romania's and Bulgaria's armies go over as three Soviet corps and their countries become Soviet-held ground: neither comes back as a nation, and nor does Yugoslavia when Belgrade is freed. Finland makes peace and keeps Helsinki, and the Lapland war against the Germans in the north is not fought. Army Group North is not attacked in Courland at all, where six battles failed to break it. Budapest is assaulted once in November and, in about half the wars, once more in December; the ring closes as news, and the city's fall in February 1945 is beyond the story's end. In a slow war a German pocket left over from 1943 at Tikhvin or Kalinin (three wars in twenty) is cleared in January 1944 with a hundred thousand prisoners or more.

Where free play stopped short when it was first built (the grand campaign has moved on since: its staffs land from the sea, and a computer's America ships its own army, Versions 72 to 75 above): the computer makes no landings from the sea of its own beyond the two Norway expeditions, so a computer-run Britain and America never invade North Africa, Italy or France, and Germany is only ever beaten from the east; a player can make those landings himself. The invasion forces history assembled for Sicily and Italy are not sent, since they belong to landings history planned. In twenty test wars to January 1946, eighteen saw war in the east: the Soviet Union was beaten in four, Germany in four, and ten were undecided. The computer's air fleets only support their armies, its research never changes, there is no negotiated peace, and the United States still enters in December 1941 whatever has happened in Europe.

**The flight model** for the air battle (`src/air/flight.ts`) reproduces the documented speed, climb and turn of the Spitfire, Hurricane, Bf 109, Bf 110, Do 17 and He 111 from their physical data, and is covered by tests.

## What is not here yet

- The gray-box air battle screen (the other half of Phase 1). The flight model, scenario data and pilot logic exist; the battle loop and the command screen do not.
- The rest of the war in the air. The computer flies air offensives only where history's plan has one, which today means Germany against Britain in 1940 and 1941, the Allies against Sicily in 1943, against the Gustav Line in the spring of 1944 and over the invasion beaches that May: it does not fly a British night offensive against Germany, does not react to a player's air offensive by moving its fighter groups, and never attempts Sea Lion itself. Support for the armies still comes from each nation's air rating, not from where its air fleets stand. There are no aircraft types, and air transport is only the one airborne assault a month. Carrier aircraft are in the grand campaign only.
- The rest of the air war as its own game. The computer's nations fly raids from the airfields their groups began the war on: they do not yet move their groups up behind a front that has advanced, so their fighters seldom escort or fight for the sky deep in enemy country, and they fly no reconnaissance or searches of the sea (their strikes on ships go for what their ships and coasts have found). A raid is one flight a month for each group, settled at once: there is no tactical air battle, no choosing of routes or heights, and no interception of a raid on its way home. A group that flew is simply away until next month. Land battles still read a nation's air strength as one number, so where the groups are based matters to raids and to patrols, not yet to the support an army gets. Airfields cannot be bombed out of use for good, captured aircraft are not kept, and there are no transport aircraft or paratroop drops planned from an airfield yet. In the historical scenarios the air war is unchanged: whole air fleets with month-long missions.
- The rest of oil and supplies. Steel, rubber, food and ammunition are one stock called supplies, not goods of their own. A nation cannot buy oil with money, lend it to an ally, or capture an enemy's depots; Lend-Lease is only the fixed lanes. Bombers cannot yet be sent against oilfields, fuel plants or tankers by name; only an agent can set a field alight. The lanes are fixed in the data: a nation cannot open a new one, and the Gulf route cannot be raided because its sea is off the map. The computer's admirals do not yet move extra escorts to a lane that is being bled or extra submarines to one worth bleeding; they keep the stations they had. Smaller countries are outside the system. The Pacific and its oil wait for the world map.
- The rest of the technology tree. Only the player's own tree is shown; an agent reading a government's books lists what its laboratories are working on, and no more. There is no stolen or captured technology, no Lend-Lease of designs, and none of the framework's superweapon projects. The tree has no nation-specific technologies: every nation researches the same 46, and what differs is where it starts and what its own abilities add. Japan is not in the game yet. A computer-run nation does not order airborne corps or carriers, so its staff leaves those two technologies for last. In the historical scenarios research is still the older system of design teams on history's clock.
- The rest of research in the historical scenarios: the computer never changes its research priorities, there is no retooling cost when a new design enters production, no captured or Lend-Lease equipment, no stolen technology, and none of the framework's superweapon projects. The United States has no design teams and Japan is not in the game yet, so Carrier Warfare and Strategic Bombing are not yet doctrines of their own.
- The rest of the war at sea: mines, and a carrier battle played out strike by strike. Sea battles are settled at once from fleet strength; there is no tactical naval battle. A fleet still works within three seas of its base and is sent to a sea at once, not moved along a course with moves to spend. A carrier wing is two groups, fighters and strike aircraft, with no torpedo bombers, dive bombers or scouts told apart; a strike stands for a month of sorties, so two carrier fleets do not trade blows within the month; and there are no escort carriers, no night strikes and no aircraft ferried to an island. The computer's admirals do not fly searches, do not split a carrier group off, and do not build carriers. Japan and the Pacific are not on the map yet, so the carrier war is so far the Atlantic and the Mediterranean. An escort guards its whole lane from wherever it stands, so a lane cannot be covered sea by sea. Submarines are counted, not played as boats: there are no wolfpacks, no codebreaking against them yet, and escort carriers are not a class of their own. The computer's admirals shell only coasts their armies stand beside and never split or join their fleets. In the history campaigns fleets keep the three older orders.
- The rest of the Mediterranean: the fighting in Syria and Iraq, East Africa, the Malta convoys, and the partisan war in Yugoslavia and Greece. Malta's garrison is supplied like any other port and its bite on the Libya convoys is one number; the only air fleet based in the Mediterranean is the Allied one formed for Sicily in June 1943, so there is no air siege of the island, and the computer never tries to take it. The computer's fleets do not fight for the Libya route.
- The rest of diplomacy: changing your own nation's side, treaties, and computer-run nations that court neutrals themselves. Today the computer follows history's events and the drift of the war.
- Italy after 1944: the last winter on the Gothic Line and the spring offensive of 1945 are not planned yet. A computer-run Italy has no plan of its own for the campaign of 1943: it defends its country by the general rules until the armistice.
- The last winter, and 1945. History's plans run to January 1945 on every front: Italy to the Gothic Line, the West to the closing of the Bulge, the East to the siege of Budapest. The Vistula offensive, the crossing of the Rhine and the end in Germany are not planned yet, and from February 1945 the computer-run nations only hold their lines. A computer-run Germany facing a player in the east in 1944 stands on the defensive and makes no counter-strokes of its own. Free play has no such end.
- Railways. Only agents sabotage a line: there are no partisans (`cutRail` in `src/campaign/rails.ts` is the hook for them). In free play the computer's air fleets fly no bombing campaigns below the General level, and above it only against the player, and the computer tears up track only when it loses ground, never by order. The railhead weakens an army in battle but does not yet slow its replacements. The historical scenarios have no lines to cut. Engineers do not build pontoon bridges for tanks in an assault: wheels and tracks wait for the bridges to be rebuilt.
- The rest of World Domination. Only the player can open it, and only once a great power has fallen and the great powers have stopped fighting each other: a German player has to beat Britain as well as France, and since the computer never lands in Britain, in a war left to the computer it is only ever the Axis that is beaten. Below the hard levels the computer makes no landings from the sea, so an island nation (Britain, Ireland) outlasts every staff on the mainland until the player lands there himself. In the test wars the Soviet Union overruns the mainland within half a year of the alliances being dissolved, with six to seven hundred divisions: the last act asks far more of a British or French player than of a Soviet one. The United States leaves instead of fighting, because its homeland is not on the map yet. There is no peace conference, there are no vassals, and there is no way to win short of being the last one standing.
- The rest of spies. The computer's nations are never fooled by the fog: their staffs see everything, they keep no agents on the map, and their services are a monthly chance of sabotage against the player only, never against each other. There is no deception yet (dummy armies, false plans), no stolen technology, no stirring of resistance, no double agents, and a caught agent is simply lost: nothing is traded for it and nothing is learned from it. Fleets at sea and air fleets are not hidden by the fog, and the forecast of a landing still counts the defenders exactly. An agent costs nothing to keep once recruited. The historical scenarios have no agents and no fog.
- The rest of difficulty. There is one setting, for the campaign; a separate setting for battles waits for the real-time battles. The historical scenarios have no levels: they are always played as history fought them. On the hard levels rivals land only against the player, never against each other or the player's allies, the landing staff looks only at harbours, and a rival's fleet does not escort its invasion: it sails when the lane is open and stays in port when it is not. The computer still plans one month at a time at every level.
- The rest of declaring war. Only the player answers to the neutrals for an attack: the computer's invasions move opinion through the course of the war, as before. A player outside both blocs (the Soviet Union) gets the surprise and pays no diplomatic price yet. The marching prompt is for ground next to the army: a landing from the sea on a neutral coast still needs war declared from the list first. Friendship does not yet bring a friend into the war, guarantee a border or change who trades with whom.
- The rest of the small countries and the League. A small country starts in September 1939 only, and the three Baltic states, Luxembourg, Slovakia, Iraq and Iran are not on offer. It has no campaign goals of its own yet. History's ultimatums to the player's country are refused for him, not put to him as a choice. A small country that joins a bloc is at war with nobody until war is declared, and the other bloc's staffs attack it only if their own plans lead there. The League is a pact of defence and nothing more: it has no war aims of its own, never goes to war as a body except when a member is attacked, cannot be left, makes no peace, and a computer-run member never invites anyone. A player outside it cannot court its members away from it.
- The rest of national abilities. The abilities are few and simple: no ability changes movement, research, diplomacy or the air war, and a special formation is a corps of an ordinary kind with an edge, not a new kind of unit with equipment of its own. Japan, China and the other nations beyond today's map have no rows yet. The computer's staff does not weigh abilities when it plans, only when it fights, and it buys its special formation whenever it can. Partisans are a monthly loss of strength, not units on the map. The numbers were set so that 72 computer wars still end every way they did before, a little nearer history: Berlin falls somewhat more often and Moscow somewhat less.
- The rest of the arsenal: a computer-run nation buys only anti-aircraft guns from it (its fleets and air forces still grow on history's programmes, and it builds no factories), nothing bought can be disbanded or sold, and works do not show on the map itself, only in the province panel. There are no raw resources yet (oil, steel, rubber), no loans or Lend-Lease, and the computer never disbands a formation it cannot pay for.
- The rest of free play: alliances the computer makes and breaks for itself, peace short of capitulation, and a staff for the air and the sea.
- The rest of the United States. In history's scenarios it is still an army in North Africa and an industry off the map. In the grand campaign it is at home, playable, and ships its own army (Versions 74 and 75) and the air forces it raises against Germany (Version 84.2), but its air forces for the Pacific wait for the Pacific, Lend-Lease is only the fixed lanes, and Mexico and Cuba have little to do once at war. Japan and the Pacific are not in the game.
- The rest of the unit stories: the computer never spends an honour, keeps a rivalry or raises a lost corps again, air fleets and fleets have no veterans-and-drafts rule of their own (pilot training lives in the air pool), admirals and air commanders have no ranks, and there are no decorations for single generals. In history's plans no Allied air fleet flies a bombing offensive yet, so the tour-of-duty honour is earned by the computer only for Germany; a player who orders bombing earns it for his own crews.
- The rest of the commander system: skills and traits for admirals and air commanders (today they gain levels from their battles, and an admiral can be lost with his flagship), command limits, rivalries and politics between generals, and staff college. Generals lead single corps today, because the prototype has no armies above the corps yet.
- The rest of towns and trade. A building cannot be pulled down or its plot given to another line, and nothing is damaged when a town is taken or bombed. Towns do not grow: a village stays a village. The buildings do not show on the map itself, only in the province panel and the town plan. A trade agreement is all or nothing: there are no terms to bargain over, no embargo, no trade in oil or supplies by agreement (those still come by the fixed lanes), and escorts on a shipping lane do not yet protect trade that crosses the same sea. The computer never ends an agreement to sign a better one.
- The rest of prepared defences. The computer's staff plans no build-up before an offensive, makes no attack whose only purpose is to wear a line down, and lands nowhere but Norway. Works belong to the corps, not the ground: a corps that relieves another starts digging afresh. There is no armistice when a war burns out. Britain, Italy and the small countries still sit most months out, and with the computer on both sides France still outlasts history by about four months. An island port fed by sea that nobody storms or cuts off (Malta) stays ringed for years.
- The rest of manpower. Men lost are lost: there are no wounded who come back, no prisoners returned and no men freed when a corps is disbanded. The reserve is one figure for a nation, so there are no colonial or allied contingents, no women in the factories and no trade-off between the army and the workforce. A small country's reserve is reckoned from the army it opens with. The Soviet staff, whose reserve is nearly always spent on replacements, still raises few guns.
- The rest of the war cabinet. Ministers are still types, not people with names and faces; the wings do not yet answer to the news (a lost capital does not strengthen the doves); a promise can name only a town, not a battle won or a fleet sunk; and the cabinet asks for generals only, never for an admiral or a minister. The cabinet still sets no directive at sea or in the air.
- The rest of sieges. A garrison cannot be taken off by sea, and a relief cannot be landed from ships straight into the field of a besieged port. Works already ordered in a province still finish while it is besieged. A town is fought for in one assault a month, not street by street. The computer still crowds more than five corps into one field when its armies fall back on a city, and its staffs do not yet starve a town out on purpose where an assault would be too dear.
- The rest of the campaign screen: government (a cabinet, policies, the mood of the country), armies above the corps with one general over several corps, and a peace conference when a long campaign is won.
- Graphics. This is deliberately a staff map. The Total War and Company of Heroes level of presentation arrives with the Unreal Engine 5 build.
- Borders are approximate: regions are grown from seed points, not traced from 1939 maps.

Everything in the simulation is deterministic: the same seed and the same orders always replay the same war. That is what will make saved games, replays and multiplayer possible, and it is why the rules can be ported to Unreal without changing their results.

## Roadmap (from the framework)

1. **Phase 1, now.** Campaign rules and auto-resolve in a browser map (done, and now deepening with production, saved games, diplomacy, the eastern front of 1941, generals, the war at sea, the war in the air, research, the Mediterranean and North Africa, Greece and the Balkans, the eastern front of 1942 and Stalingrad, the desert of 1942 and El Alamein, the United States, Torch and Tunisia, Kursk and the road to the Dnieper, Sicily and Italy in 1943, Italy in 1944, and free play from September 1939), and a gray-box air battle with 300 aircraft (paused).
2. **Phase 2.** The Battle of Britain as a vertical slice in Unreal Engine 5: one campaign turn, one playable air battle, the full Battle Ticket loop, on licensed or commissioned art. Its campaign half (radar, airfields, the aircraft and pilot pipeline, Dowding's groups under Park and Leigh-Mallory, the Channel battles and Sea Lion as a what-if) is already playable on the browser map, and its exit test passes there.
3. **Later phases.** Land battles, the war at sea, the full campaign systems, the other theatres.
